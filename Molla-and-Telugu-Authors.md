# Molla and the Other Voices: Caste, Gender and the Vernacular Counter-Current in Telugu Literary History

### From the Tāḷḷapāka Household to the Vyavahārika Movement (c. 1420 – 1950)

*A companion to "The Making of Āndhra" — that document traced Telugu's political and economic scaffolding; this one follows the poets themselves, and the fault line that runs through nearly all of them: court patronage versus a vernacular current carried disproportionately by people the court did not expect to hear from.*

---

## 0. The thesis in one breath

Telugu literary history is usually taught as a single relay race — Nannaya to Tikkana to Errana to Śrīnātha to the Aṣṭadiggajas — each runner a Brahmin or a king's salaried *vidyādhikāri*. That relay is real, but it is only **one current**. Running beside it, and periodically crossing it, is a **second current**: poets with no claim on court Sanskrit learning — **a potter's daughter, a Niyōgi Brahmin housewife, a devadāsī, a wandering ascetic of disputed caste** — who wrote in **plain, deśi Telugu**, addressed their work to **god rather than king**, and were, in nearly every case, **first honoured and then written out** of the canon by the very court culture whose language they had learned to use.

**Ātukūri Molla is the hinge figure of that second current.** A Telugu *Rāmāyaṇamu* by a Śūdra woman, composed in a register her contemporaries called embarrassingly "easy," survives four centuries of Brahmin-curated anthology-making to become one of the two or three most recited works of the language — while the court epics that eclipsed it in their own day are now read mainly by specialists. Tracing why that happened, and who her predecessors and successors in that current were, is the subject of this document.

---

## 1. The evidentiary problem — read this before trusting any date below

Literary history is **evidentially worse off than political history.** A copper plate has a regnal year; a poem usually does not.

- **Dates are reconstructed, not recorded.** Poets are dated by cross-referencing a patron named in a colophon or an internal verse against that patron's independently-dated inscriptions — which works only when a patron is named at all, and fails completely for poets (like Vēmana) who named none.
- **Hagiography fills the gap left by biography.** For low-status poets in particular, a cluster of miracle-and-vindication stories — a poetry contest won in sixty seconds, a king's Brahmin pandits struck dumb, a work at first spurned then belatedly honoured — recurs across Molla, Vēmana and others with suspicious structural similarity. These stories are **social memory about caste gatekeeping**, valuable as that, but not verifiable biography.
- **Manuscripts survived through oral and monastic transmission for centuries before print.** Vēmana's verses circulated by memory for perhaps two hundred years before anyone fixed a text; Molla's *Rāmāyaṇamu* likewise. The **printed canon most readers know today is a 19th–20th-century editorial artefact** — see §10 on C. P. Brown — not a stable object handed down unchanged from the author's own hand.
- **"Caste of the author" is often the single fact that survives** about a non-elite poet, while nearly everything else about the person is legend. That imbalance is itself a finding about how medieval Telugu society archived memory: it kept the transgression, and dropped the person.

> **Methodological rule for this document, matching its companion:** treat a poet's *dates* as a bracket, not a point; treat a *court legend* as evidence of what later generations needed the story to mean, not evidence of what happened; and do not let a poem's fame today stand in for its status in its own century.

---

## 2. The framework — mārga courts and deśi outsiders

The companion document identifies the **mārga–deśi axis** — Sanskritised pan-Indic classicism versus regional, indigenous-metre vernacular — as the organising tension of early Telugu literature. It is worth restating as the spine of *this* document too, because it maps almost exactly onto a **second axis: patronage.**

| | **Mārga current** | **Deśi / bhakti current** |
|---|---|---|
| Register | Sanskrit-saturated champū, prabandha ornament | Plain Telugu, dvipada, padam, śataka |
| Typical author | Salaried court Brahmin (*vidyādhikāri*) | Woman, artisan-caste poet, ascetic, devadāsī |
| Dedicated to | The king (legitimation, patronage fee) | God — Rāma, Kṛṣṇa, Śiva — refusing royal dedication |
| Institutional home | The royal *sabha* / Bhuvana Vijayam | Temple, household, oral/folk transmission |
| Canonical fate | Immediate prestige, later specialist readership | Initial marginalisation, later mass devotional readership |

**No poet sits purely on one side** — Śrīnātha the court professional also wrote a folk ballad (*Palnāṭi Vīra Charitra*); Kṛṣṇadēvarāya the emperor also composed a devotional prabandha for a temple deity. But the axis predicts, with real accuracy, who got a salary and who got a legend.

---

## 3. Before Molla: the currents that made her possible

*(Full detail on this stretch is in the companion document's §6; summarised here only as background.)*

- **Nannaya → Tikkana → Errana**, the **Kavitraya**, spanning Eastern Chāḷukya to Reḍḍi courts (11th–14th c.), built the **mārga** foundation: Sanskritised champū, royally commissioned, completing the *Āndhra Mahābhāratamu*.
- **Palkuriki Sōmanātha** (12th–13th c.) and **Gōna Buddha Reḍḍi**'s *Raṅganātha Rāmāyaṇamu* (the *first* Telugu Rāmāyaṇa, in dvipada) opened the **deśi** counter-current — vernacular metre, aimed at audiences the Sanskrit epics did not reach.
- **Bammera Pōtana** (c. 1450–1510) supplies the direct template for Molla: he wrote his *Āndhra Mahā Bhāgavatamu* and then, in the most celebrated act of authorial defiance in the language, **refused to dedicate it to a king**, offering it instead to Rāma. Molla's biography is, in outline, this same gesture told again — by a woman, from a lower caste, one generation later.

---

## 4. The Tāḷḷapāka household: a first woman's voice

At **Tirumala**, in the same decades, **Tāḷḷapāka Annamāchārya** (1408–1503) was composing thousands of Telugu *saṅkīrtanas* to Veṅkaṭēśvara — already noted in the companion document as the start of Andhra's devotional-musical stream.

Less often remembered is that his household produced a **second poet: Tāḷḷapāka Tirumalāmba, known as Timmakka**, his wife — a **Niyōgi Brahmin woman** credited with *Subhadrā Kalyāṇamu*, a retelling in roughly **1,170 verses** of Arjuna and Subhadrā's marriage from the *Mahābhārata*, deliberately given a **Telugu domestic and cultural colouring** rather than a literal Sanskrit rendering.

Several reference sources call Timmakka **"the first Telugu woman poet,"** predating Molla by up to a generation. The claim is genuinely contested — it depends on exactly how each poet's disputed dates are read — and is stated here as an open question, not a settled ranking. **What is not in dispute is that a temple household, not a royal court, produced this first (or nearly first) instance of a woman writing Telugu kāvya** — the same institutional pattern that will recur with Molla.

---

## 5. Ātukūri Molla

### 5.1 Life, as far as it can be reconstructed

Tradition places Molla's birth on **13 March 1440** in **Gōpavaram**, a village near Ātmakūr in what is now **Nellore district**, and her death around **1530**, aged roughly ninety — a lifespan that would run from the twilight of the Reḍḍi kingdoms (see companion document, ending 1448) through the entire Sāḷuva–Tuḷuva Vijayanagara ascent to **Kṛṣṇadēvarāya's** reign (1509–1529).

- Her father, **Ātukūri Kēśana** (Kēśana Setti), was a **potter (kummari)** of Gōpavaram and, by tradition, a **Liṅgāyat** devotee of Mallikārjuna at **Śrīśailam** — the same Śaiva pilgrimage centre whose Reḍḍi-era building programme the companion document treats in §5.3 and §2.5.
- She is popularly styled **"Kummari Molla"** — her caste folded permanently into her name, the pattern flagged in §1 as characteristic of how low-status poets are remembered.
- Tradition holds she never married, having dedicated her life and her poem to **Rāma**.

> ⚠️ **Treat the biographical dates as tradition, not archival fact.** No independently dated inscription fixes Molla's birth or death; the 1440–1530 bracket is reconstructed from internal and traditional material and should be read the way the companion document reads a dynastic praśasti — a claim, not a certified record.

### 5.2 *Molla Rāmāyaṇamu*

Her one certain work is a **Telugu retelling of the Rāmāyaṇa**, and its reputation rests on a single, deliberate stylistic choice: **radical plainness.** Where Śrīnātha and the Kavitraya reach for Sanskrit compounding as a mark of learning, Molla's Telugu is close to spoken diction, "using Sanskrit words only rarely" in the words of modern commentators — a text meant to be **heard and understood**, not admired for its difficulty.

That choice was not naive; it was a **position in the mārga–deśi argument**, taken from the deśi side, by someone the mārga establishment did not consider qualified to have an opinion at all.

### 5.3 The court legends — and why they conflict

Two incompatible traditions attach to Molla, and both are worth recording precisely because they pull in opposite directions:

1. **The honour story.** Eight court pandits are said to have tested her by setting a theme and giving her one minute to compose two verses; she did, they were "struck dumb," and conferred on her the title **"Kavi Ratna"** ("jewel among poets"), recommending the king honour her.
2. **The rejection story.** A separate strand holds that when she offered her *Rāmāyaṇamu* for dedication, Brahmin court scholars denounced it as **"śūdra-kavitvam"** — the poetry of a low-born person — and the king had male Brahmin pandits produce their own commentaries instead, sidelining hers.

> ⚠️ **This second tradition names the recipient king as "Prataparudra," which is chronologically impossible against Molla's own traditional dates** — the historical Kākatīya Pratāparudra II reigned and fell over a century before Molla was even born (see companion document §2.3–2.4). Read this strand not as a reliable event report but as **garbled social memory of caste gatekeeping**, with the king's name drifted or substituted over generations of retelling. The honour story and the rejection story may in fact be **two halves of one underlying event** — provisional acclaim followed by establishment pushback — since the same pattern (praise, then exclusion from the male Brahmin liturgical mainstream) recurs for Vēmana and for Muddupalani below.

### 5.4 Why Molla endured

The court that may have sidelined her did not control the outcome in the long run. A *Rāmāyaṇamu* written to be intelligible to people outside Sanskrit learning was **built for exactly the kind of transmission — recitation, memorisation, household and temple reading — that outlasts court fashion.** Four centuries after the Aṣṭadiggajas' elaborate prabandhas became specialist reading, Molla's plain-style *Rāmāyaṇamu* remains one of the most widely read Telugu classics — the deśi choice winning, on the very long time horizon, an argument the mārga court thought it had already settled.

## 6. The Vijayanagara zenith: the court current at its height

Molla's traditional lifetime overlaps the reign in which the **mārga, court-salaried current reached its own peak** — worth setting beside her precisely because it is her structural opposite.

**Kṛṣṇadēvarāya** (r. 1509–1529) was himself a Telugu poet: his *Āmuktamālyada*, a Vaiṣṇava prabandha narrating the story of Āṇḍāḷ (Gōda Dēvi) dedicated to Āndhra Viṣṇu of Śrīkākuḷam, opens with the often-quoted line **"Deśa bhāṣalandu Telugu lessa"** — "among the languages of the land, Telugu is the finest." Note the irony against §5: an emperor **also** dedicated his poem to a god rather than to a fellow king, but did so from inside the Sanskritised mārga register, with the full resources of empire behind him — patronage and deśi humility were not, in his case, in tension at all.

His court assembly, the **Bhuvana Vijayam** ("Conquest of the World"), housed the **Aṣṭadiggajas** — "eight elephants [of the compass]," the canonical eight court poets, each credited with at least one **prabandha** (the genre that gave classical Telugu narrative poetry its mature, ornamented form):

| Poet | Title / marker | Key work |
|---|---|---|
| **Allasāni Peddana** | "Āndhra Kavitā Pitāmahuḍu" (grandfather of Telugu poetry) | *Manucharitramu* (*Svārōchiṣa Manusaṃbhavamu*) |
| **Nandi Timmana** | "Mukku Timmana" | *Pārijātāpaharaṇamu* |
| **Mādayyagāri Mallana** | | *Rājaśēkhara Charitramu* |
| **Dhūrjaṭi** | | *Kāḷahastī Māhātmyamu* — devotional, court-skeptical in tone |
| **Ayyalarāju Rāmabhadruḍu** | | *Rāmābhyudayamu* |
| **Piṅgaḷi Sūrana** | | *Rāghava Pāṇḍavīyamu* (dual-narrative kāvya); *Kaḷāpūrṇōdayamu* — often read as Telugu's first proto-novel |
| **Rāmarājabhūṣaṇuḍu** (Bhaṭṭumūrti) | | *Vasucharitramu*, *Narasabhūpālīyamu* |
| **Tenāli Rāmakṛṣṇa** | | *Pāṇḍuraṅga Māhātmyamu* |

> ⚠️ **Do not import the folk-trickster "Tenāli Rāma" of oral comic legend into this table uncritically.** The Aṣṭadiggaja poet and the wit of popular storytelling share a name and a court, and later tradition fused them; the literary corpus (*Pāṇḍuraṅga Māhātmyamu*) is the securely attested fact, the trickster-tale cycle a separate and much-elaborated folk accretion.

This is the current Molla stood outside of — unsalaried, untitled by the court, working in a genre (itihāsa retelling) the Aṣṭadiggajas had already made to look unfashionably plain next to prabandha ornament.

## 7. The dissident countercurrent: Vēmana

If Molla represents plainness aimed at devotion, **Vēmana** represents plainness aimed at **argument** — Telugu's great **satirical-philosophical** voice, and the clearest case in the whole tradition of how badly literary dating can fail.

- His signature form is the **śataka** (century of verses) in **āṭavelaḍi** metre, each closing on the refrain **"Viśvadābhirāma Vinura Vēma"** ("Hear this, O beautiful-to-all-the-world Vēma").
- Content: blunt attacks on **caste pretension, Brahmin ritualism, idol-worship-as-performance, and social hypocrisy generally** — a populist, anti-establishment voice with no patron named anywhere in the corpus.
- **Dating is a genuine scholarly mess.** Proposed birth years scatter across **the 14th to the 18th centuries** depending on method; **C. P. Brown**, the poems' most influential modern editor, argued for **1652** on internal astrological-calendrical grounds, while other readings of the same cyclical calendar year push the birth back to **1352, 1412 or 1472.** No consensus exists, and this document takes no position beyond recording the spread.
- Vēmana's verses were transmitted **orally and in scattered manuscript for centuries** before any authoritative text existed — the same transmission gap flagged generally in §1, but sharper here than for any other poet in this document.

---

## 8. Kṣētrayya and the padam: bhakti set to music

**Kṣētrayya** (dates conventionally given as roughly 1600–1680), born in the village of **Muvva (Movva)**, Krishna district, is the major figure of the **padam** — the short Telugu devotional-erotic song, sung rather than read, addressed to **Muvva Gōpāla**, a form of Kṛṣṇa named for his own village. "Muvva Gōpāla" is his signature (*mudra*) embedded in nearly every padam rather than a second person; he is credited (almost certainly with heavy exaggeration by later transmission) with **thousands of compositions**, of which a much smaller number actually survive. His padams — voiced from a woman's (often a courtesan's) perspective addressing her divine lover — feed directly into the Carnatic musical repertoire and set the template §9's Muddupalani writes against and within.

---

## 9. Muddupalani and *Rādhikā Sāntvanam*: the century after, and its verdict

The furthest extension of the deśi-and-defiant current, a full two centuries after Molla, is **Muddupalani**, a **devadāsī** attached to the Maratha court of **Pratāp Siṃha of Thanjavur** (r. 1739–1763), writing in Telugu though based in the Tamil country — evidence of how far the language's literary prestige had travelled from Vēṅgī and Warangal (companion document, §2).

Her *Rādhikā Sāntvanam* ("The Appeasement of Rādhā," 584 verses) is framed as a dialogue between the sage Śuka and King Janaka, telling of Rādhā raising the young Ila Dēvi and then **coaching her, explicitly and physically, for her wedding night with Kṛṣṇa** — one of the earliest works in Telugu to give a woman character open sexual agency, written by a woman.

**The reception history is the real historical event here.** Muddupalani's own autobiographical preface presents her as an established, respected poet-musician-dancer in her own century — there is no contemporary record of her work being scandalous. The scandal was manufactured **later**: the text was **banned by the colonial government in 1911** on grounds of obscenity, in a campaign that also **relabelled its author as a "fallen woman"** rather than a poet. It was **not republished until 1947**, when the devadāsī-turned-scholar **Bangalore Nagarathnamma** restored it to print — an act of recovery that closes the loop opened by Molla's story: a woman author, sidelined by a male establishment claiming to speak for propriety, restored to the canon generations later by another woman.

---

## 10. The colonial hinge: C. P. Brown and the making of a printed canon

Almost everything a modern reader can access of §7–9 passes through one figure: **Charles Philip Brown** (1798–1884), a Madras Civil Service officer who became the era's most consequential **Telugu philologist** — compiling a Telugu–English dictionary, **editing and printing Vēmana's śatakas** (1829) from scattered oral and manuscript sources, and sponsoring pandits to prepare editions of the Kavitraya *Mahābhārata* and other classical texts.

This matters for method, not just trivia: the "traditional" printed Telugu canon most readers encounter today — line breaks, verse numbering, even in some cases which variant reading counts as authoritative — is substantially a **19th-century colonial-era editorial artefact**, assembled from oral and manuscript fragments by Brown and the Telugu pandits he employed. That does not make the underlying poems inauthentic, but it does mean the *fixed text* is centuries younger than the *composition* — the same foundation-versus-fabric caution the companion document applies to temple architecture (§5.3) applies here to printed poetry.

---

## 11. The modern rupture: reform prose and the vyavahārika bhāṣa movement

The mārga–deśi argument that shaped every poet above **did not end with print — it resurfaced as a direct 19th–20th-century political fight over prose.**

- **Kandukūri Vīrēśaliṅgam** (1848–1919) wrote *Rājaśēkhara Charitramu* (1878), generally reckoned **the first Telugu novel** (an adaptation of *The Vicar of Wakefield*), and led the **widow-remarriage movement**, personally arranging Andhra's first such remarriage in 1881 — social reform and vernacular prose fiction advancing together.
- **Gurajāḍa Appārāvu** (1861/62–1915) wrote ***Kanyāśulkam*** (1892; revised 1909), widely held **the greatest Telugu play** — a satire of the bride-price system, child marriage and the exploitation of Brahmin widows, and, decisively, **written in the actual spoken dialect (vyavahārika bhāṣa) of coastal Andhra** rather than the ornate literary **grandhika** register descended from mārga classicism.
- **Giḍugu Rāmamūrti** (1863–1940), Gurajāḍa's childhood friend, turned that stylistic choice into a **sustained movement**: decades of campaigning, against an entrenched pandit establishment, to make spoken Telugu — not Sanskritised grandhika — the medium of modern prose, journalism and eventually education.

**Read against §2's table, this is the mārga–deśi axis fought out again, four hundred years after Molla, with the outcome reversed:** this time the deśi register won institutionally, becoming the standard for virtually all Telugu prose written after the mid-20th century.

---

## 12. Two twentieth-century heirs, still arguing the same argument

The tension closes the twentieth century still visibly the same shape:

- **Viśvanātha Satyanārāyaṇa** (1895–1976) — **Jnanpith Award, 1970**, the first for Telugu, for *Rāmāyaṇa Kalpavṛkṣamu*; also the sprawling novel *Vēyi Padagalu* ("Thousand Hoods," 1934). A self-conscious **defender of classical form and Brahminical-traditionalist values** against the vyavahārika and progressive tides — the mārga current's most honoured 20th-century voice.
- **Śrī Śrī** (Śrīraṅgam Śrīnivāsarāvu, 1910–1983) — *Mahāprasthānam* (1950), the foundational collection of Telugu **free verse**, breaking metre entirely; a founding figure of the **Abhyudaya (progressive) writers' movement**, explicitly Marxist-inflected, radically deśi in spirit if not in the old technical sense of the word.
- A later **Jnanpith** (1988) for **C. Nārāyaṇa Reḍḍi**'s *Viśvambhara* confirms the prize kept recognising both wings of the argument across the century, not one.

**The thread Molla and Timmakka opened — a Telugu literary voice speaking from outside the court, in language meant to be understood rather than admired — resumes openly and without apology in modern Telugu women's fiction** (Ranganāyakamma, Vōlga and others), no longer needing the cover of a court legend to be heard at all.

## 13. Synthesis

1. **Two currents ran through Telugu literature from the start**, mapped exactly onto the mārga–deśi axis the companion document identifies in the Eastern Chāḷukya–Reḍḍi period: a **court, Sanskritised, male-Brahmin-salaried** current, and a **plain-Telugu, temple-or-household, outsider** current.
2. **Dedication itself became a political statement.** Potana, Molla and (in his own more complicated way) even Kṛṣṇadēvarāya all bypassed royal dedication for divine dedication — but only the low-status authors paid a reputational price for it in their own century.
3. **Caste and gender did not just shape who got to write — they shaped what got remembered about the people who did.** Molla is "Kummari Molla" before she is anything else in most sources; Muddupalani is "the devadāsī" before she is "the poet." The archive itself encodes the gatekeeping.
4. **Canon formation is a repeated, contested act, not a single moment.** Vēmana and Molla survived centuries of oral transmission before Brown and 19th-century print fixed their texts; Muddupalani was suppressed by the colonial state and had to be *re*-canonised in 1947 by another woman.
5. **The argument outlived the medieval world that started it.** The vyavahārika bhāṣa movement of Gurajāḍa and Giḍugu is the mārga–deśi fight fought again over prose rather than verse — and this time the deśi side won institutionally.

> **One-line thesis:** *Telugu literary history has never had one canon — it has had a court canon and a counter-canon running beside it, and the counter-canon's founding figure is a potter's daughter from Gōpavaram who wrote a Rāmāyaṇa in language plain enough for anyone to understand, was told by the court that this made her poetry unworthy, and outlasted the court's judgment by five hundred years.*
