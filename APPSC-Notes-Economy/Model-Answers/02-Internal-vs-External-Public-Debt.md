# Model Answer 02 — Internal vs External Public Debt (Advantages & Risks for India)

[← Model-Answers Index](README.md) · [Economy Index](../README.md) · Related: [File 02 (Public Debt)](../02-Public-Debt-Internal-and-External.md) · [File 04 §2 (Deficits)](../04-Monetary-and-Fiscal-Policy.md)

**Q. Differentiate between internal and external public debt. Explain their relative advantages and risks for a developing country like India.**

*APPSC Group-I Mains style — point-wise, data-anchored, with Andhra Pradesh integration. (~10–15 marks.)*

---

## Introduction

**Public debt** is the money a government borrows to meet the gap between its expenditure and receipts (the **fiscal deficit = borrowing requirement**). It is raised against the **Consolidated Fund** — **Art. 292** for the Union and **Art. 293** for states (states need the Centre's consent to borrow while indebted to it, **Art. 293(3)**, and **cannot borrow abroad directly**). By source it is of two kinds — **internal** (raised within the country, in rupees) and **external** (raised from abroad, usually in foreign currency). India's debt is deliberately **~95% internal and under 5% external** — a conscious post-1991 design choice.

## Differentiation — internal vs external debt

| Basis | **Internal debt** | **External debt** |
|---|---|---|
| **Source** | Domestic — banks, RBI, insurance/pension funds, public | Foreign — multilateral (World Bank, ADB, AIIB), bilateral (JICA), NRIs, commercial (ECBs) |
| **Currency** | Rupee (own currency) | Mostly foreign currency (≈54% US-dollar) |
| **Instruments** | Dated **G-secs**, **Treasury Bills**, SDLs, small savings (NSSF), Sovereign Green Bonds | Multilateral/bilateral project loans, ECBs, NRI deposits, short-term trade credit |
| **Resource effect** | **Redistribution *within*** the economy (internal transfer) | **Real resource inflow** now, real outflow (repayment) later |
| **Repayment burden** | Stays inside the country | Net burden **transferred abroad** |
| **Exchange-rate risk** | None | **Yes** — rupee depreciation raises the servicing cost |
| **Conditionality** | None | Often policy conditions / procurement ties |
| **Manager** | RBI (Centre's debt manager) via auctions | DEA, Ministry of Finance |
| **India's share** | **~95%** of central debt | **<5%** of central debt |

## Internal debt — advantages & risks

**Advantages**
- **No exchange-rate or default risk** — it is in the government's own currency; a sovereign can always service rupee debt.
- **No transfer of resources abroad** — interest is paid to domestic citizens/institutions (internal redistribution).
- Builds a **deep, liquid G-sec market** — India's biggest financial strength; supports monetary policy (SLR, OMOs) and gives banks a safe asset.
- **No external conditionality** — full policy sovereignty.

**Risks**
- **Crowding out** — heavy government borrowing pre-empts domestic savings and pushes up interest rates, squeezing private investment (File 09 §1, §8).
- **Inflation risk** if monetised (historically via ad hoc T-bills — **abolished in 1997 → WMA**, ending automatic monetisation).
- The **interest treadmill ⭐** — **interest payments are already India's single largest expenditure head (~35–40% of revenue receipts, ~₹12.8 lakh crore BE FY26)**; borrowing to pay interest violates the "golden rule."
- **Financial repression** — captive SLR demand can distort the market.

## External debt — advantages & risks

**Advantages**
- Brings in **real additional resources / foreign exchange** a capital-scarce economy cannot mobilise domestically (addresses the "deficiency of capital", File 09 §8).
- **No domestic crowding out** — it doesn't compete for local savings.
- **Concessional & project-tied** finance from multilateral agencies carries **long tenors, low rates and technical expertise** — e.g., India was the **largest IDA beneficiary (graduated 2014)**; **AIIB — India is the largest borrower**; **JICA — largest bilateral lender** (metros, bullet train).

**Risks**
- **Exchange-rate / "original sin" risk** — a falling rupee (File 09 §5) inflates the repayment burden.
- **Rollover & sudden-stop risk** — commercial borrowings (ECBs) and **short-term debt (~18–20%)** can flee in a crisis (the 2013 taper-tantrum lesson).
- **Loss of sovereignty / conditionality** — the classic memory is the **1991 BoP crisis (gold pledged, IMF conditions)**.
- Can spiral into a **debt trap** if used for consumption rather than productive assets (a warning from other developing economies).

## Which is better for a developing country like India?

- India's model — **internal debt as the mainstay, external debt only at the margin, concessional and project-tied, with no sovereign foreign-currency bonds** — is widely judged prudent.
- **Comfort indicators:** total external debt **≈$736 bn ≈19% of GDP (Mar 2025)** — moderate; **forex reserves (~$705 bn) cover 90%+ of it**; debt-service ratio ~6–7%.
- **Sustainability test — the Domar condition (r < g) ⭐:** as long as the interest rate on debt stays below the growth rate, the debt-GDP ratio stabilises. India's new **fiscal anchor targets central debt ~50±1% of GDP by 2031**.
- **Golden rule:** debt is justified when it **finances capital assets** (which generate returns to service it), not current consumption — hence the post-COVID **capex-led** strategy (multiplier ≈2.5×, File 04).

## Andhra Pradesh tie-in

- **State debt (internal):** AP's outstanding liabilities are **~30%+ of GSDP** — among the higher major states — with **off-budget borrowings via corporations/guarantees repeatedly flagged by the CAG** (a fiscal-transparency concern). Bound by **Art. 293(3)** consent and FRBM ceilings.
- **External (multilateral) debt as development strategy:** **Amaravati is financed by the World Bank ($800 mn) + ADB (~$790 mn), 2024-25**, and ADB funds the **Visakhapatnam–Chennai Industrial Corridor (VCIC)** — external project debt serviced via the Centre.
- **Contrast:** **Polavaram** is a **centrally funded national project (AP Reorganisation Act, 2014)** — a grant-type transfer, *not* debt.

## Conclusion

Internal and external debt are complementary tools: internal debt is **safe but can crowd out and burden the budget**, while external debt **brings scarce capital but carries currency and sovereignty risks**. For a developing country like India, the prudent path — which it has largely followed — is to **rely on a deep domestic debt market, use external debt selectively and concessionally, and above all borrow to build assets, keeping r < g**. Debt is then neither a sin nor a free lunch, but an instrument of development.
