# The Making of Āndhra: Political, Social, Cultural and Linguistic Transformation of the Telugu Country

### From the Eastern Chāḷukyas of Vēṅgī to the Reḍḍi Kingdoms (c. 624 – 1448 CE)

*A source-first, mechanism-first synthesis. Written to be read as a single argument, not a chronicle of kings.*

---

## 0. The thesis in one breath

Over roughly eight centuries, an **imported Deccan royal lineage** — the younger branch of the Bādāmi Chāḷukyas planted in the Krishna–Godāvari deltas — converted itself into a **deltaic agrarian monarchy**, and in the process financed three things that outlived every dynasty that built them:

1. a **Brahmanical temple-and-agrahāra order** that made forest and delta into taxable, legible, wet-rice land;
2. a **substratum of subordinate warrior lineages (sāmantas)** that repeatedly outlived their overlords and became the political raw material of the next state; and
3. a **vernacular literary culture** — Telugu made into a language of both record and high poetry.

Those three products, welded to a defensible territory, are what we mean by **"Āndhra" as a durable regional formation**. The Eastern Chāḷukyas set the machine running; the **Kākatīyas** scaled it into the first trans-regional Telugu state; the **Musunūri Nāyakas** rescued it from the Sultanate; and the **Reḍḍi kingdoms** (with their Velama rivals) carried the language and the temple economy to a courtly zenith before Vijayanagara and the Gajapatis absorbed the field.

**The single most consequential dynamic across the whole arc is linguistic: Telugu becomes a literary language on the Eastern Chāḷukyas' watch, and a courtly-classical one under the Reḍḍis.** Everything else — land, caste, religion, art — can be read as the scaffolding that made that possible.

---

## 1. The evidentiary base (read this before trusting any claim below)

Our picture is **structurally skewed**, and honest analysis has to say how.

- **Copper-plate charters (tāmra-śāsanas)** — several hundred for the Eastern Chāḷukyas alone — are the backbone. They record *land grants*: donors, boundaries, immunities, genealogies. This means we see **donative, Brahmanical, elite transactions** in high resolution and **peasants, labourers, the donated-to** barely at all. We see land given, not land worked.
- **Stone inscriptions** multiply from the Kākatīya period onward and shift the record decisively toward **Telugu**. They add **hero-stones (vīragallu)** and **sati-stones (mahāsati / mastikallu)** — our main window onto warrior society and women's deaths.
- **Literature** supplements but never replaces epigraphy: Nannaya's *Āndhra Mahābhāratamu*, Palkuriki Sōmanātha's Vīraśaiva dvipada, the *Krīḍābhirāmam* street-play of Ōrugallu, Śrīnātha's Reḍḍi-court kāvyas.
- **Foreign accounts** are an external check: **Xuanzang** (c. 639, exactly as Vishnuvardhana I consolidated) on a decaying Buddhist Andhra; **Marco Polo** (c. 1290s) on "Mutfili," the widowed queen Rudramadēvi, and the diamond mines.

> **Methodological rule for the whole document:** a grant proves an *intention to endow*, a *genealogical claim*, and a *boundary* — not a demographic fact, not a sincere theology, and not that the building we see today is the building that was founded. Foundation ≠ fabric; claim ≠ reality; ideal ≠ practice.

## 2. Political transformation — the spine of the arc

### 2.1 Eastern Chāḷukyas of Vēṅgī (624 – c. 1075): a buffer that became a substratum

**Kubja Vishnuvardhana I** (624), younger brother of Pulakeśin II, was installed as Bādāmi Chāḷukya viceroy over Vēṅgī and turned the viceroyalty into an independent line that lasted ~450 years. The **capital migrated** with the dynasty's centre of gravity:

**Pishtapura (Pithapuram) → Vēṅgī (Pedavegi, near Eluru) → Rājamahendravaram (Rajahmundry)** — the last named from Rājarāja Narendra's title *Rājamahendra*.

Those four and a half centuries were spent as a **buffer and battleground**:

- first between **Bādāmi Chāḷukyas and Pallavas**;
- then under the **Rāṣṭrakūṭas**, who reduced Vēṅgī to vassalage after Dhruva and Govinda III;
- then across the long **Chōḷa vs Western Chāḷukya of Kalyāṇī** rivalry, with Vēṅgī the perpetual prize.

The **decisive rupture** was the **27-year succession chaos after Dānārṇava (973–999)**, when **Jaṭā Choḍa Bhīma** seized Vēṅgī. Central authority never fully recovered.

**The social consequence is the key political fact of the whole period: as the centre weakened, subordinate lineages grew strong.** By the 11th–12th centuries the countryside is dotted with chiefly houses that would outlive the dynasty and become the political substratum the Kākatīyas inherited:

> Velanāṭi Choḍas · Koṭas of Dharaṇikōṭa · Haihayas of Kōna · Chāgis · Parichchhedis · Koṇḍapaḍumaṭis

This is the mechanism to hold onto: **Vēṅgī did not so much fall as decompose into its sāmantas** — and each successor state is, in part, a re-aggregation of those pieces.

### 2.2 The Chōḷa–Chāḷukya fusion and the birth of "Kulōttuṅga" (1070)

Marriage alliance did what war could not. **Vimalāditya married Kundavai** (sister of Rājarāja Chōḷa I); **Rājarāja Narendra married Ammaṅgādevī** (daughter of Rājendra Chōḷa I). Their son, born **Rājendra Chāḷukya**, ascended the **Chōḷa** throne in **1070 as Kulōttuṅga I**, founding the combined **Chāḷukya–Chōḷa line**.

What the fusion did:

- It **ended the Vēṅgī struggle** by putting both crowns on one head — bringing a long, prosperous, deliberately **inward-looking** reign (Kulōttuṅga abolished tolls: *Suṅgaṁ Tavirtta Chōḷaṉ*).
- But it **hollowed out the Vēṅgī centre**: the deltas lapsed into a **Chōḷa viceroyalty**, and **Vikramāditya VI of Kalyāṇī seized Vēṅgī in 1118**.
- The Cholas' turn away from aggressive expansion, combined with the Western Chāḷukyas' own decline, **cleared the stage for regional powers** — Hoysaḷas, Kākatīyas, Yādavas, Pāṇḍyas — to rise into the vacuum.

Culturally, though, this is **a merger, not an ending**: Chōḷa temple idiom, administrative vocabulary and Tamil Śaiva devotionalism flowed **north**; Telugu-speaking warrior lineages flowed **south** into Chōḷa and later Vijayanagara service. The circulation is itself part of how "Āndhra" was consolidated.

### 2.3 Kākatīyas of Ōrugallu (c. 1163 – 1323): the first Telugu-land state

The Kākatīyas are the hinge of the whole arc — the moment the Vēṅgī substratum **re-aggregates into a sovereign Telugu polity**.

- **Origin as feudatories** (of Rāṣṭrakūṭas, then Western/Eastern Chāḷukyas). Sovereignty dates only to **Prōla II / Rudradēva** — *do not date the dynasty's independence to its origin.*
- Centre moved **Anmakoṇḍa (Hanamkoṇḍa) → Ōrugallu (Warangal, Ēkaśilā Nagaram)**.
- **Rudradēva → Mahādēva → Gaṇapatidēva** (the great expander and integrator) **→ Rudramadēvi → Pratāparudra II**, under whom Warangal fell to the Delhi Sultanate in **1323**.
- **The Kākatīya varṇa was contested** — records point to **fourth-varṇa / Śūdra origins with subsequent Kṣatriya-isation** through inscriptional genealogy. This is the textbook case of *status mobility manufactured in epigraphy*.

Two Kākatīya innovations reshaped the country materially and are treated in §3: the **tank-irrigation revolution** and the **Motupalli trade charter**.

### 2.4 The Sultanate interregnum (1323 – c. 1336) and the Musunūri *reconquista*

- **1323:** Ulugh Khan (later Muḥammad bin Tughlaq) takes Warangal; Pratāparudra II is captured (dies en route). Brief, disruptive **alien rule** — the moment Islam and Indo-Islamic institutions enter the Telugu country as a permanent factor.
- **c. 1325–1368: the Musunūri Nāyakas.** **Prōlaya Nāyaka** and then **Kāpaya (Kāpanēḍu) Nāyaka** organised a **confederacy of Telugu nāyakas** (tradition: seventy-odd chiefs) and **expelled the Sultanate from Warangal (c. 1336)** — a genuine *reconquista* and a rare instance of coordinated Telugu-warrior political action.
- The confederacy's afterlife matters more than its brief supremacy: it **legitimised a new class of self-made warrior-chiefs** — Reḍḍis, Velamas, and others — as the successor sovereigns. The same years (1336) see **Vijayanagara** founded on the Karnataka flank, the other great inheritor of the anti-Sultanate energy.

### 2.5 The Reḍḍi kingdoms (c. 1325 – 1448) — the named terminus of this arc

The Reḍḍis are where the story lands. They are the clearest demonstration of the whole thesis: **a non-Kṣatriya, agrarian-caste warrior lineage builds a sovereign Telugu kingdom and pours its surplus into the Telugu language and the Śaiva temple.**

**Foundation and the Vilasa moment.**
**Prōlaya Vēma Reḍḍi** (r. c. 1325–1353), of the **Panta Reḍḍi** clan, established the kingdom with its capital first at **Addaṅki**, then the great hill-fort of **Koṇḍavīḍu**. His **Vilasa copper-plate grant** is a set-piece source: it paints the **disorder of the Turuṣka (Tughlaq) invasion** — Brahmins dispossessed, agrahāras destroyed, *varṇāśramadharma* overturned — and casts Prōlaya Vēma as the **restorer** who re-founded Brahmin settlements and re-established order. Whatever its rhetorical inflation, it captures the **self-image of the Reḍḍi state as dharmic restoration after alien disruption.**

**The dynasty and its split into two lines:**

| Ruler | Approx. reign | Seat | Marker |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Prōlaya Vēma Reḍḍi** | c. 1325–1353 | Addaṅki → Koṇḍavīḍu | Founder; Vilasa grant; patron of **Errana** |
| **Anavōta (Anapota) Reḍḍi** | c. 1353–1364 | Koṇḍavīḍu | Consolidation |
| **Anavēma Reḍḍi** | c. 1364–1386 | Koṇḍavīḍu | Greatest territorial extent; **Śrīśailam steps / maṇḍapa** works |
| **Kumāragiri Reḍḍi** | c. 1386–1402 | Koṇḍavīḍu | Reign under which the **Rājamahendravaram branch** hives off |
| **Peda Komaṭi Vēma Reḍḍi** | c. 1402–1420 | Koṇḍavīḍu | Scholar-king; patron of **Śrīnātha** (as *vidyādhikāri*) |
| **Rācha Vēma** | c. 1420–1424 | Koṇḍavīḍu | Kondavidu line ends; absorbed into the Vijayanagara–Gajapati contest |

**The Rājamahendravaram (Rajahmundry) branch** grew from **Kāṭaya Vēma Reḍḍi**, brother-in-law and governor under Kumāragiri, and ran through **Allāḍa Reḍḍi** and finally **Vīrabhadra Reḍḍi** (patron of Śrīnātha in his later years), ending **c. 1448** as the **Gajapatis of Orissa** and Vijayanagara squeezed the deltas from north and south.

**Why the Reḍḍis matter for the transformation thesis:**

1. **Legitimation without Kṣatriya blood.** The Reḍḍis are an *agrarian-caste* dynasty who rule as full sovereigns — the endpoint of the "manufactured Kṣatriya / status-mobility" theme that runs from the Kākatīyas onward. Sovereignty in medieval Andhra had become **achievable through arms, land-restoration and Brahmin patronage**, not birth.
2. **Court patronage as the engine of classical Telugu.** The Reḍḍi courts of Koṇḍavīḍu and Rājamahendravaram are where Telugu poetry goes fully **courtly** (see §6): **Errana** at Prōlaya Vēma's court completing the Kavitraya's *Mahābhārata*; **Śrīnātha**, "Kavi Sārvabhauma," as a salaried minister of learning.
3. **Śaiva temple statecraft.** The Reḍḍi kings' signature monuments are at **Śrīśailam** — the **Vīraśiromaṇḍapam**, the **flight of steps and the Pātāḷagaṅga path** — fusing pilgrimage, Śaiva-Ārādhya affiliation and royal display.

**The Rēcherla Velamas of Rāchakoṇḍa and Dēvarakoṇḍa** are the necessary counter-lineage: Kākatīya-descended warrior chiefs, bitter **rivals of the Reḍḍis**, whose fort-based principalities and the genealogy in the *Vēlugōṭivāri Vaṁśāvaḷi* show the same pattern — self-made Telugu warrior houses partitioning the post-Kākatīya field.

## 3. The agrarian and economic substratum — because social life rests on it

**The engine of the entire transformation is agrarian expansion, not conquest.** Sanskritisation and state-formation in the Telugu country moved at the speed of the plough and the tank.

### 3.1 Land grants as a state-building device

Two grant types recur from Vēṅgī through the Reḍḍis:

- **Agrahāra / brahmadēya** — villages to Brahmin corporations (often *chaturvedins*), with fiscal and judicial **immunities (parihāra)**.
- **Devadāna** — land to temples.

The best reading of what these *did* (Hermann Kulke's **processual / integrative model**) is that the king **inserted Brahmanical settlements into tribal and forest tracts to convert them into taxable, legible agrarian space.** The grant is not a symptom of fragmentation but an **instrument of expansion**. Its social by-products:

1. a **landed Brahmin gentry** in the deltas, distinct from the older **Buddhist-mercantile** order of the Krishna valley (Amarāvatī, Ghaṇṭaśāla, Nāgārjunakoṇḍa);
2. the **temple as a corporate landholder, employer, bank and school** — the institutional ancestor of the Kākatīya- and Reḍḍi-era temple economy;
3. the extension of **wet-rice cultivation with tank-and-channel irrigation** into deltaic and upland-forest margins.

### 3.2 The Kākatīya tank revolution and the temple as a total institution

Under the Kākatīyas, **tank-building became a royal-pious duty** — the hydraulic core of the state. **Pākāla, Laknavaram, and the Rāmappa Cheruvu** are the monuments of a water-managed agrarian order; the **Ayagār** village-servant system organised local service around it.

The medieval Andhra temple, correspondingly, was a **total institution**: landholder (*devadāna*), employer (priests, devadāsis, musicians, artisans, accountants), **bank and redistributor**, and school (attached *ghaṭikās*). **This is why temple-building was inseparable from statecraft** — a temple was simultaneously an economic corporation, a poor-relief system, and a legitimation theatre.

### 3.3 Trade, guilds and the Motupalli charter

The delta faced the sea, and the state protected the trade:

- **Motupalli** (Krishna coast) was the premier Kākatīya port. **Gaṇapatidēva's *Abhaya Śāsanam*** (charter of safety, c. 1240s) **fixed customs duties, guaranteed the security of foreign merchants and their goods, and even renounced the customary royal seizure of shipwrecked cargo** — a strikingly pro-commerce measure. Marco Polo's diamond trade (the Golconda / Kollūr region) flowed through this coast.
- Trans-regional merchant guilds — the **Nānādēśi / Ayyāvoḷe (Ainūrruvar)** — and local **nakaram** bodies knit the ports to the interior. **Craft guilds**, weavers foremost (**Padmaśāli / Sāle**), anchored a monetised economy in which gold *gadyāṇa / varāha* (pagoda) coins circulated.

### 3.4 The Nāyaṅkara horizon

Though it flowers fully under Vijayanagara, the **nāyaṅkara / amaranāyaka** logic — military commanders holding **revenue assignments (amaram)** in return for maintaining troops — is visible in embryo in the **Musunūri confederacy and the Reḍḍi/Velama chieftaincies**. It is the fiscal-military grammar into which the post-Kākatīya warrior lineages settled.

## 4. Social transformation — caste, community, women

### 4.1 From lineage-and-office to a lattice of jātis

**A crucial correction against anachronism.** In **Eastern Chāḷukya** inscriptions, people are identified chiefly by **locality, lineage (gotra / kula), office and title** — *nāyaka, rāṣṭrakūṭa, boya, senāpati* — and only secondarily by caste name. The *chāturvarṇya* ideal is recited (the king's duty to preserve *varṇāśramadharma*), but the operative identities are **status-and-occupation-based**, not the fixed caste blocs of later centuries.

> ⚠️ **Do not project Kamma / Velama / Reḍḍi / Kāpu caste blocs back onto Vēṅgī.** Cynthia Talbot's work on Kākatīya Andhra (*Precolonial India in Practice*, 2001) shows these as **fluid, status-and-occupation identities in the 12th–13th centuries**, which hardened much later — some of it a product of colonial-census categorisation.

By the **Kākatīya–Reḍḍi** centuries, however, the record shows a **multiplying lattice of jātis**:

- **Brāhmaṇas** increasingly split into **Vaidika** (ritual) vs **Niyōgi** (the "secular," administrative Brahmins who staffed revenue and clerical offices) — a distinction that hardens in exactly this medieval Andhra milieu.
- **The "Kṣatriya problem":** genuine Kṣatriya lineages were thin, so ruling houses **manufactured solar/lunar genealogies** in their praśastis. The Kākatīyas' contested varṇa and the sovereign **agrarian-caste Reḍḍis** are the two ends of this trajectory — *status mobility achieved through arms and epigraphy.*
- **Mercantile castes** — the **Kōmaṭi (Ārya Vaiśya)**, whose community-formation myth of **Vāsavi Kanyakā Paramēśvari** (the mass immolation at **Penugoṇḍa** to escape a lustful Chāḷukya king) simultaneously indexes **merchant assertiveness and the sati ideal**.
- **Artisan and service jātis** — **Padmaśāli / Sāle** weavers, Kammari smiths, the **Kaṁsāli / Viśvabrāhmaṇa** five artisan groups claiming Brahmin status (*Pañcāṇamvāru*), potters, oil-pressers.

### 4.2 The right-hand / left-hand moieties (Valaṅgai / Iḍaṅgai)

Society was cross-cut by two supra-caste **moieties** — **Valaṅgai (right-hand,** broadly agriculturist**)** and **Iḍaṅgai (left-hand,** broadly artisan/mercantile**)**, each notionally "18 castes." Their recorded disputes were **about ritual honours and symbolic privileges** (processional insignia, palanquins, the conch) — **not economic class.** This is the clearest evidence that **medieval social conflict was often about honour and rank, not material class.**

### 4.3 The central paradox of women's position

The evidence pulls hard in two directions and must not be flattened:

- **Constraining side:** entrenched patriarchy; **sati** attested archaeologically (*mahāsati* stones) among warrior/elite families; **child marriage** and widow restrictions rising; institutionalised **devadāsi** service and courtesan quarters (the courtesan streets of Ōrugallu, lovingly detailed in the *Krīḍābhirāmam*); royal polygamy.
- **Enabling / exceptional side:**
  - **Royal women as autonomous donors** run right through the arc. The earliest and most instructive is **Ayyaṇa Mahādevī**, chief queen of Kubja Vishnuvardhana I, who granted **Musinikoṇḍa to the Jaina temple Nadumbi-vasadi at Bezawāḍa (Vijayawada)** — *a Śaiva-affiliated court, a Jaina endowment, a female donor,* collapsing several lazy generalisations in one record.
  - **Rudramadēvi (r. c. 1262–1289)** — the outstanding datum. Installed via the **putrikā** device, she **ruled as a male king** under the style **"Rudradēva Mahārāja,"** was independently praised by **Marco Polo**, and **died in battle** against the Kāyastha chief Ambadēva. A queen-*regnant*, not a regent.
  - **Women poets** — **Ātukūri Molla** (a potter-caste woman, Telugu *Rāmāyaṇamu*, usually placed slightly later) and the female voices of the **Tāḷḷapāka** family.

> **Framing line:** the period shows **elite-conditional female agency** — a queen could rule as a king, yet the same society normalised sati and the devadāsi institution. Agency was a function of **dynasty and rank, not a general social advance.**

### 4.4 Everyday life and bondage

The *Krīḍābhirāmam* window onto Ōrugallu — bustling bazaars and guild streets, **temples as social hubs**, **toddy and non-vegetarian fare** beside Brahminical vegetarianism, gambling, cock-fights, dance and music — confirms that **religious pluralism and dietary diversity coexisted in a single city.** Beneath it lay **domestic and agrestic dāsa/dāsī bondage**: sale and gift of persons is recorded, including donation of servitors to temples, and bonded agrarian labour underpinned the tank-irrigated economy.

## 5. Religious transformation — from heterodoxy to a competitive temple-bhakti order

The long-run story is the **eclipse of Buddhism and Jainism** and the rise of a **competitive, temple-centred Śaiva–Vaiṣṇava bhakti order**, with a **distinctively Andhra sectarian formation — Ārādhya Śaivism — as the signature contribution.**

### 5.1 Syncretic legitimation, inherited from Bādāmi

Nearly every Eastern Chāḷukya plate recites the dynastic formula: of the **Mānavya gotra**, **Hāritīputras**, nourished by the **Sapta Mātṛkās**, protected by **Kārttikeya (Svāmi-Mahāsena)**, having obtained the **Vārāha lāñchhana** by Nārāyaṇa's grace. Note what this welds together: a **mother-goddess cult**, a **war-god cult**, and a **Vaiṣṇava boar emblem** onto a Brahmanical gotra claim. **Legitimation was syncretic by design** from the start.

### 5.2 The heterodoxies decline

- **Buddhism**, once brilliant at Amarāvatī–Dhānyakaṭaka and Nāgārjunakoṇḍa, was **already residual by Xuanzang's visit (c. 639)** — he reports monasteries mostly deserted. The land-grant regime finished what economic shift had begun: **endowment flowed to Brahmins and temples, not saṅghas.**
- **Jainism** enjoyed **early and intermittent royal favour** and then faded under Śaiva pressure. The Eastern Chāḷukya record is explicit about the patronage: **Ayyaṇa Mahādevī's Bezawāḍa grant**; **Amma II (Vijayāditya VI)'s Kaluchumbarru grant** to the Jaina teacher Arhanandin; **Vimalāditya's Ranastipuṇḍi grant** recording his initiation by the Jaina preceptor **Trikālayogi Siddhāntadeva**. By the Kākatīya period the **Vīraśaiva and Ārādhya movements were explicitly, sometimes violently, anti-Jaina**, and basadis were being converted or abandoned.

> ⚠️ **Correction of a common error:** the Eastern Chāḷukyas were **not "purely Śaiva."** Jaina and Vaiṣṇava endowments run throughout.

### 5.3 Śaivism — dominant and internally plural

Most Eastern Chāḷukya kings style themselves *Parama-Māheśvara*; **Vijayāditya II** is credited (ideologically, not by census) with **108 battles and 108 Śiva temples (Narendreśvara)**. Over the medieval centuries Śaivism layers into a spectrum from orthodoxy to radicalism:

1. **Pāśupata, Kāḷāmukha, Kāpālika** — the older ascetic/tantric streams; the **Kāḷāmukhas controlled maṭhas and temple establishments** (notably around Śrīśailam) and ran centres of learning.
2. **Vīraśaivism / Liṅgāyatism** — spilling in from Karnataka (**Basava**, 12th c.): rejection of Vedic ritualism, caste hierarchy and image-worship in favour of the personal **iṣṭaliṅga**, the wandering **jaṅgama**, and social egalitarianism.
3. **★ Ārādhya Śaivism — the distinctively Andhra synthesis.** The **Ārādhyas were Telugu *Brāhmaṇa* Vīraśaivas** who **accepted iṣṭaliṅga devotion while *retaining* Vedic ritual, the sacred thread and Brahminical caste identity** — precisely the elements Kannada Vīraśaivism *rejected.* Andhra **domesticated the radical Karnataka movement into a caste-conserving, Veda-affirming form.** Fountainhead: **Mallikārjuna Paṇḍitārādhya** (12th c., *Śivatattvasāramu*). Its great vernacular voice is **Palkuriki Sōmanātha**, whose *Basava Purāṇamu* and *Paṇḍitārādhya Charitramu* in the **dvipada** couplet metre were a **conscious act of religious democratisation through vernacular verse** (see §6).
4. **Śrīśailam (Mallikārjuna)** — a **pan-Indian node**: simultaneously a **Jyotirliṅga** and a **Śakti Pīṭha (Bhramarāmba)**, a magnet for Kāḷāmukha, Vīraśaiva and Ārādhya activity — and, tellingly, **the flagship building project of the Reḍḍi kings** (the Vīraśiromaṇḍapam and the steps).
5. The older **Pañchārāma** network — **Amarārāma (Amarāvatī), Drākṣārāma, Kṣīrārāma (Palakollu), Kumārārāma (Sāmalkōṭ), Sōmārāma (Bhīmavaram)** — Chāḷukya-era **foundations** that remained live pilgrimage anchors.

> ⚠️ **Foundation ≠ fabric.** The standing structures at several Pañchārāma sites carry heavy **later Chōḷa, Velanāṭi, Kākatīya and even 18th-century** work (Vāsireḍḍi Veṅkaṭādri Nāyuḍu at Amarāvatī). Eastern Chāḷukya *foundation* is not Eastern Chāḷukya *building.*

### 5.4 Vaiṣṇavism — Rāmānuja's long shadow

**Śrī Vaiṣṇavism of Rāmānuja** (traditional dates c. 1017–1137; read the ~120-year span as hagiographic, effectively 11th–12th c.) reshaped Andhra Vaiṣṇavism:

- **Doctrine — Viśiṣṭādvaita** ("qualified non-dualism"): the one Brahman (**Nārāyaṇa**) is *qualified* by sentient souls (*cit*) and insentient matter (*acit*), related to Him as **body to soul** (*śarīra-śarīri-bhāva*); liberation by **bhakti** and, distinctively, **prapatti / śaraṇāgati** (total self-surrender). Canon: *Śrī Bhāṣya*, *Vēdārtha-saṅgraha*, *Gītā-bhāṣya*.
- **Twin scripture — *ubhaya-Vēdānta*:** authority granted to **both** the Sanskrit śruti **and** the Tamil ***Nālāyira Divya Prabandham*** (the 4,000 hymns of the twelve **Āḻvārs**) — carrying a **vernacular-devotional charge** alongside Vedic orthodoxy. Lineage: **Nāthamuni → Yāmunāchārya → Rāmānuja.**
- **Deccan spread via the exile mechanism:** persecution by a Śaiva Chōḷa king (tradition names *Kulōttuṅga I / "Krimikaṇṭha"* — **the identification is itself disputed**) drove Rāmānuja into the **Hoysaḷa** country, seeding the sect across the Deccan; its Andhra anchors became **Tirumala, Śrīkūrmam** and later **Ahōbilam**.
- **Śrīkūrmam** (Śrīkākuḷam) — a rare **Kūrma-avatāra** shrine whose tradition dates a **Śaiva→Vaiṣṇava re-affiliation to Rāmānuja (c. 1130)**; but its dense epigraphy also carries a **Dvaita imprint** — **Narahari Tīrtha**, a direct disciple of **Madhva**, left inscriptions here — so the tidy "Rāmānuja converted it" story is **sectarially contested.** A model source-critical caveat.
- **Social edge:** the tradition is remembered for **inclusive** gestures (proclaiming the esoteric *Tiru-mantra* to all from the temple tower; regard for outcaste devotees, *Tirukkulattār*), giving Andhra Śrī Vaiṣṇavism a **bhakti-driven social-widening** dimension.
- The sect later split into **Vaḍagalai** (Sanskrit-Veda leaning) and **Teṅgalai** (Tamil-prabandham leaning); **Madhva Dvaita** gained ground under later patronage.

Major Vaiṣṇava kṣetras that mature in this period: **Tirumala–Tirupati (Veṅkaṭēśvara)**, **Ahōbilam (Nava-Narasiṁha)**, **Siṁhāchalam (Varāha-Narasiṁha,** Chandanōtsavam**)**, **Śrīkūrmam.** The devotional-musical stream begins with **Tāḷḷapāka Annamāchārya (1408–1503)** and his thousands of Telugu **saṅkīrtanas** at Tirumala.

### 5.5 Śakta, folk cults, and the arrival of Islam

Beneath the "great tradition" sects lived **grāma-dēvatā** worship (Pōlēramma, Māridamma), the **Bhramarāmba** Śakta cult at Śrīśailam and **Māṇikyāmba** at Drākṣārāma, and **vīra / hero cults** (the **Palnāḍu** heroes; the **Kāṭamarāju** cattle-epic). The **1323 Tughlaq conquest** then introduced **Islam and Indo-Islamic institutions** as a permanent neighbour — running from **military-ideological antagonism** (the Musunūri "liberation") to **pragmatic accommodation** in courts and warfare. The synthesis is real but should not be romanticised past the evidence.

## 6. Linguistic transformation — the rise of Telugu (the heart of the matter)

This is where the Telugu country earns its place in cultural history. Trace it as **three stages**: Telugu enters the *record*, then *poetry in stone*, then *the courtly-classical literature* of the Kākatīya–Reḍḍi centuries.

### 6.1 Stage one — Telugu enters the public record (Eastern Chāḷukya plates)

Vēṅgī's copper plates are **bilingual in structure**: **Sanskrit does the ideology** (the *praśasti* and the imprecatory verses), **Telugu does the land** (the boundary descriptions and the operative details of the grant). That functional division is the seed of everything: Telugu establishes itself first as the language of **fact and locality**, Sanskrit reserved for **prestige and cosmology**.

- The **Addaṅki inscription of Pāṇḍaraṅga** (c. 848 CE), a general of Guṇaga Vijayāditya III, carries **Telugu verse in the *taruvōja* metre** — among the earliest Telugu poetry in stone.
- The **Bezawāḍa inscription of Yuddhamalla II** (early 10th c.) gives Telugu **madhyākkara / sīsa**-type verse.
- Across this period the **Telugu script differentiates from the shared Telugu–Kannada script**, largely settling by the 11th century.

> ⚠️ **Do not misattribute the "first."** The **earliest Telugu inscription proper is the Kalamalla / Erragudipāḍu record of the Renāṭi Choḍas (c. 575 CE)** — Kadapa region, **not Vēṅgī.** Vēṅgī's claim is to the first *literary* Telugu in stone, not the first Telugu.

### 6.2 Stage two — Nannaya and the birth of Telugu literature

**Nannaya Bhaṭṭāraka**, court poet of the Eastern Chāḷukya **Rājarāja Narendra (1022–1061)**, began the **Āndhra Mahābhāratamu** — the founding act of Telugu literature. Precise, hedged facts:

- He completed the **Ādi** and **Sabhā** parvas and **part of the Araṇya** parva — **not the whole Mahābhārata.** Titles: **Ādikavi**, **Vāganuśāsanuḍu** ("legislator of speech"). Assisted by **Nārāyaṇa Bhaṭṭu.**
- His medium is **champū** (mixed verse and prose), heavily **Sanskritised** — the ***mārga*** (pan-Indic, classical) register.
- **Motive matters:** for a king of **contested descent and Chōḷa marriage-ties**, rendering the Mahābhārata into the subjects' language was an **act of legitimation**, not mere patronage of belles-lettres.
- The Sanskrit grammar of Telugu, the ***Āndhra Śabda Chintāmaṇi***, is **traditionally** ascribed to Nannaya — **an attribution seriously disputed** and best treated as later and retro-ascribed.
- A contemporary of the milieu, **Pāvulūri Mallana**, rendered Mahāvīrāchārya's *Gaṇitasārasaṅgraha* into Telugu — a rare **vernacularisation of a scientific, not devotional, text.**

**The organising axis of all early Telugu literary history is the *mārga–deśi* tension** — the pull between the **Sanskritised, pan-Indic classical register (mārga)** and the **regional, indigenous-metre impulse (deśi:** *dvipada* and the like**)**. That tension is **set up under Vēṅgī** and worked out over the next four centuries.

### 6.3 Stage three — the Kavitraya completed, and the deśi counter-current

The *Mahābhārata* project spanned the whole political arc, binding the dynasties into a single literary lineage — the **Kavitraya (Trinity)**:

| Poet | Period / patron | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| **Nannaya** ("Ādikavi", "Vāganuśāsanuḍu") | 11th c., **Rājarāja Narendra** (E. Chāḷukya) | Ādi, Sabhā, part of Araṇya; the *mārga* foundation |
| **Tikkana** ("Kavi Brahma", "Ubhaya-kavi-mitra") | 13th c., **Manuma Siddhi** (Nellore Telugu Chōḷa) | 15 parvas (Virāṭa→Svargārōhaṇa); *Nirvachanōttara Rāmāyaṇamu* |
| **Errana / Yerrāpragada** ("Prabandha Paramēśvara") | 14th c., **Prōlaya Vēma Reḍḍi** (Koṇḍavīḍu) | **Completed the fraught Araṇya Parva**; *Nṛsiṁha Purāṇamu*, *Harivaṁśamu* |

> Note the arc: the trinity **begins at an Eastern Chāḷukya court and ends at a Reḍḍi court** — the literary spine of this entire document. (The legend of Nannaya's "curse" leaving the Araṇya Parva incomplete is **literary tradition, not documented fact** — flag it.)

Meanwhile the **deśi** current ran in parallel and reached non-elite audiences deliberately:

- **Gōna Buddha Reḍḍi** — *Raṅganātha Rāmāyaṇamu*, the **first Telugu Rāmāyaṇa**, in **dvipada** (Kākatīya era).
- **Baddena** — *Sumati Śatakam*, gnomic *nīti* verse.
- **Palkuriki Sōmanātha** — Vīraśaiva **dvipada** as democratised scripture (§5.3).

### 6.4 Stage four — the Reḍḍi court makes Telugu courtly-classical

The Reḍḍi kingdoms are where Telugu poetry becomes a **prestige court art in its own right**, no longer subordinate to Sanskrit:

- **Śrīnātha** ("**Kavi Sārvabhauma**", c. 1365–1441) — salaried ***vidyādhikāri*** (minister of learning) at the **Koṇḍavīḍu** court of **Peda Komaṭi Vēma Reḍḍi**, and later at **Rājamahendravaram** under **Vīrabhadra Reḍḍi.** His range defines the age: *Śṛṅgāra Naiṣadhamu* (rendering Śrīharṣa), *Bhīmēśvara Purāṇamu*, *Kāśīkhaṇḍamu*, *Haravilāsamu*, *Śivarātri Māhātmyamu*, and the ballad ***Palnāṭi Vīra Charitra*** ("Āndhra Kurukṣetram"). His legend — poetic duels, courtly fame, a fall into hardship — became part of Telugu cultural memory. **The salaried court-poet is itself the marker of Telugu's arrival as a language of power.**
- **Bammera Pōtana** (c. 1450–1510, straddling the Reḍḍi twilight) — *Āndhra Mahā Bhāgavatamu*; famous for **refusing to dedicate it to a king, offering it instead to Rāma** — a defining assertion of **literary-devotional independence** from the very court patronage that had raised the language.

The Reḍḍi achievement thus completes the linguistic transformation: from **Telugu-for-boundaries** on a Vēṅgī plate to **Telugu as the medium of a sovereign court's self-expression.** (The classical *zenith* — Kṛṣṇadēvarāya's *Āmuktamālyada* and the Aṣṭadiggajas of the *Bhuvana Vijayam* — lies just beyond this arc, under Vijayanagara, and is its direct continuation.)

## 7. Art and architecture — a crossroads becoming a school

Vēṅgī sat at a **stylistic crossroads** — Chāḷukyan (Vesara-leaning), Pallava/Drāviḍa from the south, Kaliṅga from the north — and the region gradually developed **distinct idioms**.

- **Eastern Chāḷukya — Biccavōlu** (ancient Birudaṅkarāyapuram, East Godāvari): the finest surviving group — **Golingeśvara**, Rājarājeśvara and Chandraśekhara temples (9th–10th c.) — celebrated for **iconographic richness** (Ardhanārīśvara, Dakṣiṇāmūrti, Sūrya, Agni, Chāmuṇḍā). **Drākṣārāma Bhīmeśvara** shows a **two-storeyed vimāna within a double prākāra** — an Eastern Chāḷukya conception with later accretions.
  > ⚠️ The **Undavalli and Mogalrājapuram caves** near Vijayawada are **commonly mis-assigned** to the Eastern Chāḷukyas; mainstream dating puts the principal excavation in the **Vishṇukuṇḍin** era (4th–5th c.), with later reworking.
- **Kākatīya — a genuinely distinct school:** the **Rudrēśvara (Rāmappa) Temple, Palampēṭ** (built 1213 by Rēcherla Rudra; **UNESCO World Heritage, 2021**) — a **stellate jagati**, exquisite madanikā bracket-figures in polished black **dolerite**, and the famous **light "floating" bricks** of the śikhara; the **Thousand-Pillar Temple, Hanamkoṇḍa** (1163, a **trikūṭālaya**); and the **Kākatīya Kalā Tōraṇam** of Warangal Fort, now the **State emblem of Telangana.**
- **Reḍḍi & Velama phase — predominantly military architecture:** the great **hill-forts of Koṇḍavīḍu, Koṇḍapalli, Rāchakoṇḍa, Dēvarakoṇḍa**, plus **temple additions at Śrīśailam and Ahōbilam.** The Reḍḍi signature is not a temple *style* so much as **pilgrimage infrastructure** — the Śrīśailam **Vīraśiromaṇḍapam** and the ghat-steps — and **fortification**, fitting a warrior polity holding a contested frontier.
- **The Vijayanagara horizon** (beyond this arc but its heir): the ornate pillared maṇḍapas, rearing *yāḷi* piers and towering rāya-gōpurams at Hampi, and — the **Andhra face of the school** — the **Lēpākṣi Vīrabhadra temple** (hanging pillar, Nāgaliṅga monolith, the finest surviving Vijayanagara **murals**).

## 8. Synthesis — how "Āndhra" was made

Reading the four transformations together:

1. **Political:** an imported Deccan viceroyalty **decomposed into sāmantas**, which the Kākatīyas **re-aggregated into the first sovereign Telugu state**, which the Sultanate broke and the **Musunūri–Reḍḍi–Velama** generation **rebuilt as achievement-based warrior kingdoms.** Sovereignty migrated from **birth to arms, land-restoration and Brahmin patronage.**
2. **Economic:** **land grants + tank irrigation + the temple-as-corporation + protected coastal trade** turned forest and delta into a **legible agrarian-commercial order** — the material base that every dynasty taxed and every temple redistributed.
3. **Religious:** the **heterodoxies were extinguished** and a **competitive temple-bhakti order** installed, whose signature Andhra move was the **domestication of radical Karnataka Vīraśaivism into caste-conserving Ārādhya Śaivism**, beside a socially-widening Śrī Vaiṣṇavism.
4. **Linguistic:** the through-line — **Telugu rose from the boundary-clauses of Vēṅgī's plates to the courtly-classical poetry of the Reḍḍi assembly halls**, along the enduring *mārga–deśi* axis first set up under the Eastern Chāḷukyas.

**When the Kākatīyas and then the Reḍḍis built Telugu polities, they built on Vēṅgī's foundations** — its sāmanta lineages, its temple economy, its literary language. The **Vēṅgī-maṇḍala / Āndhra-deśa** identity — *territory + Telugu + Śaiva temple network* — is the durable formation that all of this produced.

> **One-line thesis:** *Under the Eastern Chāḷukyas an imported Deccan lineage became a deltaic agrarian monarchy and, in financing the Brahmanical temple order, the sāmanta chiefdoms, and the vernacular literary culture, laid down "Āndhra" — a formation the Kākatīyas made sovereign and the Reḍḍis made classical.*
