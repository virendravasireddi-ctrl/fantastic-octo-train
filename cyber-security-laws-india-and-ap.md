# Cyber Security Laws in India and Andhra Pradesh

A detailed reference covering the **substantive law**, the **procedural machinery**, and the **problems/criticisms** — at the national (India) level and for the State of **Andhra Pradesh (AP)**.

> **Disclaimer:** This is an educational overview, not legal advice. Cyber law in India is changing quickly (IT Act rules, the DPDP Act 2023, and the 2023–24 replacement of the IPC/CrPC/Evidence Act by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita / Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita / Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam). Verify current provisions and notifications before relying on any point.

---

## Part A — The Legal Framework in India

India does not have a single consolidated "Cyber Security Act." Instead, cyber security and cybercrime are governed by a **patchwork** of statutes, rules, and sectoral regulations.

### 1. The Information Technology Act, 2000 (IT Act) — the core statute

Enacted to give legal recognition to electronic transactions (originally modelled on the UNCITRAL Model Law on E-Commerce) and later heavily amended by the **IT (Amendment) Act, 2008** to add cybercrime and cyber-security provisions.

**Key substantive provisions (offences and liabilities):**

| Section | Subject |
|--------|---------|
| **43** | Civil liability — damage to computer, unauthorised access, downloading, introducing viruses, denial of service; compensation payable. |
| **43A** | Body corporate failing to protect **sensitive personal data** — liable to pay compensation for negligence. |
| **65** | Tampering with computer **source documents/code**. |
| **66** | Computer-related offences (dishonestly/fraudulently doing acts under s.43) — up to 3 years / fine. |
| **66B** | Dishonestly receiving stolen computer resource / device. |
| **66C** | **Identity theft** (misuse of password, digital signature, other unique ID). |
| **66D** | **Cheating by personation** using a computer resource (phishing, fake profiles, online fraud). |
| **66E** | Violation of **privacy** — capturing/publishing private images. |
| **66F** | **Cyber terrorism** (threat to unity, integrity, security, sovereignty; unauthorised access to protected systems). Punishable up to **life imprisonment**. |
| **67 / 67A / 67B** | Publishing/transmitting **obscene**, **sexually explicit**, or **child sexual abuse material (CSAM)** in electronic form. |
| **69** | Power to **intercept, monitor, decrypt** information (subject to the 2009 Interception Rules). |
| **69A** | Power to **block** public access to information/websites. |
| **69B** | Power to **monitor and collect traffic data** for cyber security. |
| **70** | **Protected systems** (Critical Information Infrastructure). |
| **70A / 70B** | **NCIIPC** (Critical Infrastructure Protection) and **CERT-In** (national incident response agency). |
| **72 / 72A** | Breach of confidentiality and privacy; disclosure of information in breach of a lawful contract. |
| **79** | **Intermediary safe harbour** — conditional immunity for platforms, subject to "due diligence." |
| **84B / 84C** | Punishment for **abetment** and **attempt**. |

**Struck-down provision:**
- **Section 66A** (offensive messages) was declared **unconstitutional** in *Shreya Singhal v. Union of India* (2015) for violating Article 19(1)(a) (free speech) — it was vague and overbroad. (Notably, cases were still being *registered* under it for years afterwards, prompting the Supreme Court to issue further directions.)

### 2. Rules and subordinate legislation under the IT Act

- **IT (Reasonable Security Practices and Procedures and Sensitive Personal Data or Information) Rules, 2011** ("SPDI Rules") — obligations on bodies corporate handling sensitive personal data (largely superseded in principle by the DPDP Act, but still referenced).
- **IT (Procedure and Safeguards for Interception, Monitoring and Decryption of Information) Rules, 2009.**
- **IT (Procedure and Safeguards for Blocking for Access of Information) Rules, 2009.**
- **IT (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021** (amended 2022/2023) — grievance officers, traceability of the "first originator" for messaging, takedown timelines, and a digital media / OTT code.
- **CERT-In Directions of 28 April 2022** — mandatory **incident reporting within 6 hours**, log retention (180 days), KYC/record retention for VPNs, data centres, cloud, and crypto exchanges, and clock synchronisation to NIC/NPL time.

### 3. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act)

India's first standalone **data protection** law (received assent Aug 2023; rules being finalised). Key features:
- Applies to processing of **digital personal data**; concepts of **Data Fiduciary**, **Data Processor**, **Data Principal**, and **consent**.
- **Consent Managers**, rights of access/correction/erasure/grievance, obligations of notice and purpose limitation.
- **Significant Data Fiduciaries** with extra obligations (DPO, audits, impact assessments).
- **Data Protection Board of India** for adjudication; penalties up to **₹250 crore**.
- Cross-border transfer allowed except to blacklisted countries; broad **government exemptions**.

### 4. General criminal law (IPC → Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023)

Many cyber offences are charged **alongside** general penal provisions:
- Cheating, forgery, criminal breach of trust, defamation, criminal intimidation, extortion, obscenity, stalking, voyeurism.
- With the **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS)** replacing the **Indian Penal Code, 1860**, these map to new BNS sections (e.g., cheating, forgery of electronic records, organised crime, terrorism). Practically, FIRs now cite **BNS + IT Act** together.

### 5. Sectoral and other regulations

- **RBI** cyber-security framework for banks, NBFCs, payment operators; **digital lending** guidelines.
- **SEBI** cyber-security & cyber-resilience framework (CSCRF) for market intermediaries.
- **TRAI / DoT** telecom security; the **Telecommunications Act, 2023**.
- **IRDAI**, **NPCI/UPI** rules, **Aadhaar Act, 2016** (biometric/identity data).
- **Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In)** operational directions.

## Part B — Procedural Aspects (How Cyber Cases Actually Work)

### 1. Investigation authority
- Under s.**78 of the IT Act**, offences are investigated by an officer **not below the rank of Inspector** of Police (raised from DSP by the 2008 amendment).
- **Cyber Crime Police Stations** and **cyber cells** at state/district level; the central **Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre (I4C)** under MHA coordinates nationally.
- **National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal (cybercrime.gov.in)** and helpline **1930** for online-fraud reporting; the **Citizen Financial Cyber Fraud Reporting and Management System** enables quick freezing of fraudulent transactions.

### 2. Jurisdiction
- **Section 75** gives the IT Act **extra-territorial** reach — it applies to offences committed outside India if the act involves a computer/network **located in India**.
- Cyber offences are location-agnostic, so **territorial jurisdiction** (which police station / court) is frequently contested; FIRs may be filed where the victim resides, where the accused is, or where the server/effect is located.

### 3. Classification of offences (arrest / bail / trial)
- The **Schedule** to the IT Act and the **CrPC/BNSS** determine whether an offence is **cognizable/non-cognizable** and **bailable/non-bailable**.
- Serious offences (66F cyber terrorism, 67B CSAM) are **cognizable and non-bailable**; several s.66-family offences are **bailable**.
- **Section 77B** (added 2008): offences punishable with **3 years or more** are **cognizable**; those below are **bailable**.

### 4. Adjudication (civil/compensation track)
- **Adjudicating Officer** (Secretary, IT, of the State) decides claims under **s.46** where damages **do not exceed ₹5 crore**; above that, the civil court.
- Appeals lie to the **Telecom Disputes Settlement and Appellate Tribunal (TDSAT)** — the erstwhile **Cyber Appellate Tribunal** was merged into TDSAT.

### 5. Electronic evidence
- Governed historically by **Section 65B of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872** (now **Section 63 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023**): electronic records are admissible only with a **certificate** attesting to the manner of production and integrity.
- Case law: *Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer* (2014) made the 65B certificate mandatory; *Shafhi Mohammad* (2018) relaxed it; **Constitution Bench in *Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal* (2020)** settled that the **certificate is mandatory** (with narrow exceptions where the original device is produced).
- **Chain of custody**, **hash values**, **forensic imaging**, and **seizure procedure** for digital devices are recurring litigation points.

### 6. Interception, blocking and takedown
- **Interception/monitoring/decryption** — s.69 + 2009 Rules (competent authority is the Union/State Home Secretary; a review committee reviews orders).
- **Website/URL blocking** — s.69A + 2009 Blocking Rules (confidential process, criticised for opacity; upheld in *Shreya Singhal* subject to safeguards).
- **Intermediary takedown** — s.79 + 2021 Rules; **actual knowledge** now generally requires a **court order or government notification** (per *Shreya Singhal*), with strict timelines for specified content.

### 7. Prosecution & trial
- Trial before designated **magistrate/sessions courts**; some states designate special cyber courts.
- Delay, weak forensic capacity, and evidentiary technicalities frequently affect conviction rates.

## Part C — Andhra Pradesh (AP): State-Level Position

Policing is a **State subject**, so while the **substantive law is central** (IT Act, BNS, DPDP), **enforcement, cyber-security governance of state IT systems, and specialised units are handled by the State**.

### 1. Institutional / enforcement setup in AP
- **AP Cyber Crime Police Stations / Cyber Cells** in each district and at the CID (Crime Investigation Department) level; a state **Cyber Crime Coordination Centre** aligned with the national **I4C**.
- **AP CID Cyber Crimes wing** handles major online-fraud, financial-fraud, and organised-cybercrime investigations.
- Participation in the **1930 helpline** and **cybercrime.gov.in** for citizen reporting and rapid transaction-freezing.
- Emphasis in recent years on **cyber-fraud recovery**, awareness drives, and cyber forensic labs.

### 2. AP e-governance & state cyber-security governance
- AP runs large **e-governance / digital-service** platforms (e.g., citizen service portals, land records, welfare/DBT systems, the state's real-time governance stack). These make **security of state IT infrastructure** a major concern.
- **APCERT-type / state SOC (Security Operations Centre)** functions and **State Data Centre** security are managed via the state's IT department / IT agency, generally aligned to CERT-In directions and MeitY guidelines.
- The state has periodically announced **cyber-security policies/initiatives** and MoUs (with academia and industry) for capacity building, cyber forensics, and skilling.

> **Note on specifics:** AP's institutional names, the exact current cyber-security policy document, and the number/locations of cyber police stations change with administrations and reorganisation (including the post-2014 bifurcation from Telangana). Confirm the **current** notifications from the AP Home Department / AP IT & Communications Department before citing exact figures.

### 3. What is *State* vs *Central* in AP
- **Central:** the offences and their definitions (IT Act, BNS, DPDP), interception/blocking powers, CERT-In, I4C coordination.
- **State (AP):** registering FIRs, investigation, prosecution, cyber police infrastructure, forensic labs, awareness, and **securing the state government's own digital systems** and data.

## Part D — Problems and Criticisms

### 1. Substantive / legal-design problems
1. **No single, coherent cyber-security statute.** The law is fragmented across the IT Act, DPDP Act, sectoral regulators, BNS, and rules — creating overlaps, gaps, and confusion.
2. **Outdated core statute.** The IT Act 2000 (last major amendment 2008) predates cloud, IoT, AI, ransomware, deepfakes, crypto, and modern data flows. A proposed **Digital India Act** to replace it has been discussed for years but not enacted.
3. **Vague/overbroad drafting.** Section **66A** was struck down for vagueness; concerns persist about broad terms in intermediary rules and blocking powers enabling over-censorship.
4. **Weak, delayed data-protection regime.** The DPDP Act arrived only in 2023, is **not yet fully operational** (rules pending), and is criticised for **wide government exemptions**, dilution of the RTI Act, limited data-principal remedies, and absence of provisions on harms like profiling/automated decision-making.
5. **Deemed-consent / notice weaknesses and cross-border ambiguity** under DPDP.

### 2. Procedural / enforcement problems
1. **Jurisdictional confusion.** Because cybercrime is borderless, victims are often **shuttled between police stations**; determining the right jurisdiction delays FIR registration.
2. **Low registration and reluctance to file FIRs.** Police sometimes discourage complaints or misclassify offences.
3. **Capacity and skills gap.** Shortage of trained cyber investigators, forensic experts, and updated labs; large pendency and **low conviction rates**.
4. **Digital-evidence technicalities.** Rigid **65B / Sec 63 BSA certificate** requirements, chain-of-custody failures, and improper seizure often sink prosecutions.
5. **Slow international cooperation.** Cross-border evidence relies on **Mutual Legal Assistance Treaties (MLATs)**, which are slow; India is not a party to the **Budapest Convention** on Cybercrime.
6. **Speed of fraud vs. speed of response.** Even with helpline 1930, **money is frequently withdrawn/layered** before accounts can be frozen; recovery rates remain modest.
7. **Under-reporting of breaches** by companies despite CERT-In's 6-hour rule; and compliance burden/criticism of those very directions (e.g., **VPN log/KYC mandates** raising privacy concerns).

### 3. Rights and oversight concerns
1. **Surveillance without robust judicial oversight.** Interception (s.69) and blocking (s.69A) are executive-driven, with **secret proceedings** and limited transparency; the review mechanism is internal to the executive.
2. **Opaque website blocking.** Blocking orders are confidential, so users and even platforms may not know why content was blocked, weakening remedies.
3. **Intermediary rules (2021) tensions.** **Traceability** of the "first originator" is argued to break end-to-end encryption; expanded takedown obligations raise free-speech and privacy concerns and are under challenge in courts.
4. **Chilling effect on speech** from vague provisions and pressure on intermediaries.

### 4. State-level (including AP) problems
1. **Uneven capacity across districts** — cyber police stations concentrated in cities; rural complainants underserved.
2. **Coordination friction** between state police, banks, telecom operators, and national bodies (I4C, CERT-In) slows takedowns and fund-freezing.
3. **Security of large state e-governance/welfare databases** — concentration of citizen data (Aadhaar-linked, DBT, land) raises **breach and misuse risks**; state SOCs are still maturing.
4. **Dependence on central definitions** — states cannot easily adapt offence definitions to local fraud patterns; they can only build enforcement capacity.
5. **Awareness gap** — a large share of victims (elderly, first-time internet users, rural populations) fall to phishing/UPI/loan-app frauds; awareness campaigns lag behind attacker innovation.

## Part E — Quick Reference Summary

| Layer | India (Central) | Andhra Pradesh (State) |
|-------|-----------------|------------------------|
| **Core offence law** | IT Act 2000/2008; BNS 2023 | Applies same central law |
| **Data protection** | DPDP Act 2023 (rules pending) | Applies + secures state data systems |
| **Security agencies** | CERT-In (70B), NCIIPC (70A), I4C | State cyber cells, CID Cyber Crimes, state SOC |
| **Reporting** | cybercrime.gov.in, helpline 1930, CERT-In 6-hr rule | District cyber PS + national portal |
| **Evidence** | Sec 63 BSA 2023 (ex-65B Evidence Act) | Same |
| **Appeals (civil)** | Adjudicating Officer → TDSAT | AP IT Secretary as Adjudicating Officer |
| **Main problems** | Fragmentation, outdated IT Act, weak oversight, low conviction | Capacity gaps, coordination, e-gov data security, awareness |

## Key Cases to Know
- *Shreya Singhal v. Union of India* (2015) — struck down s.66A; read down 69A/79.
- *K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017) — **privacy is a fundamental right** (foundation for data-protection law).
- *Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer* (2014) & *Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Gorantyal* (2020) — electronic evidence / 65B certificate.
- *K.S. Puttaswamy (Aadhaar)* (2018) — limits on Aadhaar/data use.
