## 0. How to use these notes (and how to read the evidence)

Mains answers are graded on **argument, chronology, source-awareness and balance**, not on the number of names you can list. So every section below is built the same way:

1. **The core narrative** — what happened, in the right sequence, with the dates that matter.
2. **The evidence** — what *kind* of source tells us this (archaeology, inscription, coin, text, foreign account), and therefore what we can and cannot claim.
3. **The analysis / debates** — the interpretive fault-lines an examiner expects you to know.
4. **Answer hooks** — compressed one-liners you can drop into an answer.

> **The single methodological rule for the whole document:** the *type* of evidence determines the *kind* of claim you may make. Archaeology gives material culture but rarely names or motives; inscriptions give an official self-image, not a neutral fact; texts give ideals and later redactions, not photographs of their own age. State your source, and you have already half-written a good answer.

**The spine of the whole arc in one breath:** India moves from **foraging bands** → the subcontinent's **first urbanism (Harappa)** → its **de-urbanised, pastoral-agrarian, Sanskritic re-foundation (Vedic)** → the **second urbanisation and territorial states (Mahājanapadas)** → the **heterodox religious revolution (Jainism, Buddhism)** that both reflected and challenged that new society → the **first pan-Indian empire (Mauryas)** → and, after its fall, an era of **foreign ruling houses (Indo-Greeks to Kushāṇas)** that turned India's north-west into a cosmopolitan crossroads — **while in the Deccan an indigenous power, the Sātavāhanas, rose as the first great empire of the south and the seedbed of Andhra history.** Hold that skeleton; everything else hangs on it.

## 1. Pre-Historic Cultures in India

**Definition.** "Pre-history" = the human past **before writing**, reconstructed almost entirely from **material remains** — stone tools, bones, hearths, rock art. Because there are no texts, everything here is **archaeological inference**. India has **no fossil of an early hominin as old as Africa's**, so the Indian story begins with **artefacts, not skeletons**.

### 1.1 The periodisation (learn this table cold)

| Age | Approx. Indian span | Diagnostic technology | Economy | Type-sites |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Palaeolithic (Old Stone Age)** | c. 2 million – 10,000 BCE | Core tools; hand-axes, cleavers (Lower); flake tools (Middle); blades & burins (Upper) | Hunting–gathering; nomadic | Bhimbetka (MP), Hunsgi (Karnataka), Attirampakkam (TN), Didwana (Raj.), Kurnool caves (AP), Soan valley |
| **Mesolithic (Middle Stone Age)** | c. 10,000 – 6,000 BCE | **Microliths** (tiny blades, hafted) | Hunting–gathering + incipient herding; first evidence of **domestication** | Bagor (Raj.), Langhnaj (Guj.), Bhimbetka, Adamgarh (MP), Sarai Nahar Rai (UP) |
| **Neolithic (New Stone Age)** | c. 7,000 – 1,000 BCE (regionally staggered) | **Polished** stone tools; food **production** | Farming + animal domestication; settled villages; pottery | **Mehrgarh** (Baluchistan — the keystone), Burzahom & Gufkral (Kashmir), Chirand (Bihar), Koldihwa & Mahagara (UP), Piklihal, Maski, Brahmagiri, Hallur (S. India) |
| **Chalcolithic (Copper–Stone)** | c. 3,000 – 1,000 BCE | Copper + stone together | Village farming; painted pottery; often **pre/para-Harappan or post-Harappan** | Ahar–Banas & Kayatha (Raj./MP), Malwa, **Jorwe** (Maharashtra — Daimabad, Inamgaon), Chirand |

*Note the overlaps:* the ages are **technological stages, not calendar boxes**. A Neolithic economy in the south is contemporaneous with the Bronze-Age Harappan cities in the north-west. This regional staggering is itself an exam point.

### 1.2 What each stage actually tells us

- **Palaeolithic.** Robert Bruce Foote (1863, Pallavaram near Madras) found India's first recognised Palaeolithic tool — the discipline's Indian starting-gun. **Bhimbetka** (Madhya Pradesh) is the single richest site: continuous occupation from Lower Palaeolithic onward, and **~500+ rock-shelters** whose **paintings** (mostly Mesolithic and later) depict hunting, dancing, animals — our earliest window onto belief and social life. The **Soan (Sohan) valley** industry (now Pakistan) defines the northern pebble-tool tradition; the peninsular tradition is hand-axe based (Acheulian). **Attirampakkam** has pushed the Indian Lower Palaeolithic back dramatically (Acheulian ~1.5 million years, some claims older) — cite it to show currency with recent research.
- **Mesolithic.** The technological marker is the **microlith**. The economic marker is the **beginning of domestication**: **Adamgarh** and **Bagor** give the earliest evidence of domesticated animals. The first **human burials** with grave goods appear (Mahadaha, Sarai Nahar Rai in the Ganga plain) — a hint of ritual and belief. Rock art peaks.
- **Neolithic — the real revolution.** V. Gordon Childe's "**Neolithic Revolution**" = the shift from **food-gathering to food-production**. Its Indian keystone is **Mehrgarh** (c. 7000 BCE, on the Bolan/Kachi plain), the earliest farming village in the subcontinent and the **direct ancestral horizon of the Harappan tradition** — wheat, barley, cattle, later cotton and mud-brick houses. Regional Neolithics have signatures worth naming: **Burzahom** (Kashmir) — **pit-dwellings** and the famous **dog-buried-with-master**; **Chirand** (Bihar) — bone tools; the **southern ash-mound** sites (Piklihal, Utnur) — cattle-pastoralism. Koldihwa (UP) is cited for early **rice**.
- **Chalcolithic.** First systematic use of **metal (copper)**. Cultures are **regional and painted-pottery based** (Ahar/Banas black-and-red ware; Malwa; **Jorwe**). Crucially, many are **contemporary with or successor to the Harappans but technologically humbler** — they show that "Bronze-Age urbanism" was a north-western phenomenon, while much of India remained village-Chalcolithic. **Daimabad** (Jorwe, Maharashtra) yielded the famous **bronze chariot/animal hoard** — the southernmost Harappan-linked find. **Inamgaon** is the best-excavated Chalcolithic village (proto-urban planning, granary, evidence of social differentiation and its eventual decline through aridity).

### 1.3 Debates & answer hooks

- **"India has artefacts but not the hominins"** — frame Indian pre-history as a record of **cultures, not creatures**; we infer people from their tools.
- **Continuity thesis:** Mehrgarh → mature Harappa is the strongest case for an **indigenous, in-situ evolution** of the first cities — a key weapon against "civilisation-was-imported" models.
- **Rock art (Bhimbetka)** is the earliest evidence of **cognition, ritual and aesthetics** — use it when a question asks about "art and religion in early India."
- **Regional staggering** = the ages are **stages of technology, not universal dates**; the south's Neolithic overlaps the north's Bronze Age.

### 1.4 How we know — dating the deep past (a source-note)

Because pre-history has **no texts**, every date here is a **scientific or relative inference**, and naming the method is itself a source-criticism mark examiners reward:

- **Relative dating** — **stratigraphy** (deeper = older), **typology** (evolving tool-forms in sequence), and **faunal/floral association**. These give *order*, not calendar years.
- **Absolute dating** — **radiocarbon (¹⁴C)** for organic remains up to ~50,000 years (the technique that fixed the Harappan mature phase); **potassium–argon** for the very deep Palaeolithic; **thermoluminescence** for fired clay; **dendrochronology** for wood; and now **ancient DNA** (Rakhigarhi) for population history.
- **The caution to write:** dates are **ranges with error-bars, not points**, and the **stage-not-date rule** (§1.1) means "Neolithic" names a *way of life*, not a year — which is exactly why the south's Neolithic can be contemporary with the north's Bronze Age.

## 2. The Indus Valley (Harappan) Civilization

**The claim to fame:** the subcontinent's **first urbanisation** — a **Bronze-Age**, **literate**, **city-based** civilization, and, at its height, the **largest of the ancient world by area** (from Sutkagendor on the Makran coast to Alamgirpur near Delhi; from Manda in J&K to Daimabad in Maharashtra). It is one of the "big three" river-valley civilizations alongside Egypt and Mesopotamia.

### 2.1 Chronology & the discovery

- **Phases:** Early/Pre-Harappan (c. 3300–2600 BCE) → **Mature Harappan** (c. **2600–1900 BCE**, the urban climax) → Late Harappan / de-urbanisation (c. 1900–1300 BCE).
- **Discovery:** brought to light in **1921 (Harappa, Daya Ram Sahni)** and **1922 (Mohenjo-daro, R.D. Banerji)**, under **John Marshall**, ASI. Radiocarbon later fixed the mature phase. The name is geographic (**Indus**) but the civilization is **not confined to the Indus** — a majority of sites cluster on the now-dry **Ghaggar-Hakra (identified by many with the "Sarasvatī")**, hence "**Indus-Sarasvatī**" in some usage. Naming it after the type-site, "**Harappan**," is the safest academic convention.

### 2.2 The key sites (name-drop with a reason, never in a bare list)

| Site | Modern location | Signature finds — *what it proves* |
|---|---|---|
| **Harappa** | Punjab (Pak.) | Type-site; granaries; cemetery R-37; workmen's quarters |
| **Mohenjo-daro** | Sindh (Pak.) | **Great Bath**, Great Granary, **"Priest-King"** bust, **bronze "Dancing Girl,"** Pashupati seal — the archetype of the planned city |
| **Dholavira** | Gujarat (Rann of Kutch) | **Three-part** city; monumental **water reservoirs**; the **10-sign signboard**; stadium — Harappan hydraulic engineering at its peak |
| **Lothal** | Gujarat | **Dockyard** (tidal); bead factory; rice husk; fire-altars — the **port and manufacturing** face |
| **Kalibangan** | Rajasthan (Ghaggar) | **Ploughed field** (earliest attested), **fire-altars**, evidence of an **earthquake** |
| **Rakhigarhi** | Haryana | **Largest** Harappan site by area (in India); aDNA studies |
| **Banawali, Rangpur, Surkotada, Chanhudaro, Sutkagendor, Alamgirpur, Daimabad** | various | Regional extent; Surkotada — the debated **horse bones**; Chanhudaro — the only site with **no citadel**, a bead-making town |

### 2.3 Urbanism — the analytical core (this is what Mains tests)

- **Town planning:** the **grid pattern** (streets at right angles); the **citadel (raised west) + lower town (east)** division — signalling an ordered, possibly stratified society. **Standardised, kiln-fired bricks** in a fixed **ratio (4:2:1)** across a thousand kilometres — the strongest single proof of **shared standards and central regulation**.
- **Water & sanitation** — the civilization's true genius: covered **drains**, house-to-street drainage, wells, the **Great Bath** (a ritual tank?), Dholavira's reservoirs, Lothal's dock. **No other Bronze-Age civilization matched Harappan sanitation.**
- **Economy:** wheat, barley, and the world's earliest **cotton** ("*sindon*" to the Greeks); an internal + external trade network — **seals and Harappan goods in Mesopotamia**, which called a trading partner "**Meluhha**." Standardised **weights** (binary/decimal, chert cubes) → a regulated commercial economy. **Craft specialisation:** beads (carnelian), shell, faience, metallurgy, but **iron was unknown** (a Bronze Age).
- **Script & seals:** the **undeciphered** Harappan script (~400+ signs, **boustrophedon**, right-to-left), known mainly from **steatite seals** (unicorn, bull, the "**Pashupati**"/proto-Śiva seal). Because it is **undeciphered, we cannot read names, gods, or history** — the single biggest constraint on all interpretation.
- **Religion (inferred, never attested):** Mother-Goddess figurines; the **Pashupati seal** (a horned figure among animals — read by Marshall as proto-Śiva, disputed); **pipal-tree** and animal veneration; possible fire-worship (Kalibangan/Lothal altars). **No monumental temples, no identifiable royal tombs** — hence debate over the polity.
- **Polity — the great puzzle:** **who ruled?** No palaces, no royal burials, no depiction of kings or armies, few weapons. Interpretations range from a **priestly oligarchy**, to a **merchant-managed** order, to a **stateless / heterarchical** system of shared elite norms. The honest Mains line: *the uniformity implies strong coordination, but its political form is unknown because the script is unread.*

### 2.3a Society, art & the "Bronze-Age markers" (high-yield add-ons)

- **Social structure — differentiated, yet not "royal":** house-size variation, the **citadel/lower-town** divide and unequal burials point to **ranking and occupational specialisation**, yet the **absence of palaces, royal tombs or grand temples** yields the "**faceless**," probably **heterarchical or priest-mercantile** society (§2.3) — *not* a flaunted god-king monarchy on the Egyptian model.
- **Art — intimate, not imperial:** the masterpieces are **small-scale** — the bronze **"Dancing Girl"** (lost-wax casting), the steatite **"Priest-King,"** the naturalistic **bull and "Pashupati" seals**, terracotta **Mother-Goddesses**, and the earliest **dice and gamesmen**. **Monumental sculpture is absent** — a defining contrast with contemporary Egypt and Mesopotamia, and a clue to the polity.
- **The "markers" to state precisely (examiner-bait):** the Harappans *had* **bronze, cotton, the wheel, the potter's wheel, standardised weights and writing**; they *lacked* **iron, the horse (the Surkotada bones are debated), the true arch, and coined money** — the exact profile of a **Bronze-Age** civilisation, and a ready checklist for the Indus-vs-Vedic comparison (§3).

### 2.4 The decline — a menu, not a single cause

No single cause is accepted. Marshal the candidates and **conclude with multi-causality**:

1. **Environmental/climatic:** weakening monsoon; **drying of the Ghaggar-Hakra (Sarasvatī)** through river capture/tectonics — the strongest current favourite, since so many sites sat on it.
2. **Tectonic/flood:** repeated flooding at Mohenjo-daro; earthquake evidence at Kalibangan.
3. **Ecological:** deforestation for brick-kilns, over-exploitation, salinity.
4. **Aryan invasion (Wheeler's "massacre at Mohenjo-daro"):** the **skeletons** he read as invasion victims are now **discredited** as a decline-cause — they are of different periods and show no battle pattern. **De-emphasise; treat as a debunked hypothesis.**
5. **Systemic de-urbanisation:** trade collapse (Mesopotamian links dried up) + these stresses → cities were abandoned but **rural Late-Harappan life continued** (Cemetery H, Jhukar, Rangpur). **The civilization transformed and dispersed rather than vanishing.**

### 2.5 Debates & answer hooks

- **Continuity, not extinction:** Harappan traits (ritual bathing, mother-goddess, pipal, weights, terracotta) **filter into later Indian culture** — argue for a *legacy*, not a dead end.
- **The Aryan question:** the **relation between the Harappans and the Vedic Aryans** is the hinge debate — see §3.4. Ancient DNA from **Rakhigarhi** is the newest evidence and should be cited carefully (it complicates simple invasion/migration narratives).
- **"A civilization without a face":** the undeciphered script means we know **how they lived but not what they thought, said, or were called** — the defining limitation.
- **Uniformity vs. autonomy:** standardised bricks/weights/script argue for integration; the **absence of monumental kingship** argues against a centralised empire. Present both.

### 2.6 Origin and expansion — the answer-frame the exam wants *(2025 Q1a: "Discuss the origin and expansion of the Indus Valley Civilization")*

A "origin and expansion" question is not asking for a site list; it asks for a **narrative of how the first cities were *born* and how far and why they *spread*.** Frame it in two moves:

- **Origin — indigenous, gradual, agrarian (not an import).** Trace the pre-urban roots: **Mehrgarh (c. 7000 BCE)**, the Bolan-plain farming village, → the **Early / Pre-Harappan cultures (c. 3300–2600 BCE)** — Hakra ware, Ravi, **Kot Diji, Amri, Sothi–Siswal, Kalibangan-I** — which already show **fortified settlements, mud-brick housing, wheel-made pottery and standardised crafts** → coalescing into the **Mature Harappan c. 2600 BCE.** The argument to sell: the civilization was an **in-situ evolution out of the Mehrgarh farming tradition**, *not* a colonial diffusion from Mesopotamia (Wheeler's older "idea of civilization came from the west" is now rejected). **Mehrgarh → mature Harappa is the origin story.**
- **Expansion — the largest of the Bronze-Age world.** From the nuclear Indus and **Ghaggar-Hakra (Sarasvatī)** plains it spread across **~1.3–1.5 million sq km and 1,500+ sites**, bounded by **Sutkagendor** (Makran coast, west) – **Alamgirpur** (near Delhi, east) and **Manda** (J&K, north) – **Daimabad** (Godāvarī, Maharashtra, south). The **engines of expansion**: **agrarian surplus + long-distance trade + resource procurement** (Khetri copper, Gujarat carnelian, Afghan lapis, coastal shell) welded together by a **shared "great tradition"** — the 4:2:1 bricks, the binary-decimal weights, the seals and script — the material glue that let one culture stretch a thousand-plus kilometres.
- **Answer frame in one breath:** *origin = indigenous, evolutionary, Mehrgarh-rooted; maturation into planned cities c. 2600 BCE; expansion = the widest spread of any Bronze-Age civilization, integrated by standardisation and trade* — then qualify that **regional "domains" (Sindhi, Kutch–Saurashtra, Ghaggar, Baluchistan)** persisted beneath the uniformity.

### 2.7 Survival and continuity into the present — the Harappan legacy *(2023 Q1a: "Examine the survival and continuity of Harappan culture in the present times")*

This question demands a specific, counter-intuitive thesis: **the Harappan world did not simply "end" in 1900 BCE — it de-urbanised, dispersed, and seeped into the living Indian tradition.** Argue on two registers — **(i) archaeological continuity** (the Late-Harappan/Chalcolithic successors of §2.4) and **(ii) cultural survivals traceable to the present** — then close with the source caveat.

| Domain | Harappan feature | Survival / echo today |
|---|---|---|
| Religion | **Pashupati / "proto-Śiva" seal**; horned deity; lingam-like stones | **Śiva** worship, **linga-yoni** cult, **Nandi** (humped bull) |
| Religion | **Mother-Goddess** terracottas | **Śakti / grāma-devatā** (village-goddess) worship, still ubiquitous in the Deccan and south |
| Religion | **Pipal (aśvattha)**, animal and tree veneration | Sacred pipal/banyan, **nāga** and tree shrines |
| Ritual | **Great Bath**; fire-altars (Kalibangan, Lothal) | Temple **tanks**, river-**ghāṭ** bathing, Kumbh; **homa/yajña** fire ritual |
| Ritual | Seated figure in a **yoga-like posture** on seals | Yoga/āsana tradition *(argued, cautiously)* |
| Symbol | **Swastika** and endless-knot motifs | Auspicious swastika in daily and ritual use |
| Adornment | Bangles; a figurine with **vermilion in the hair-parting** | Bangles; **sindūr** in the parting of married women |
| Craft | **Carnelian/agate bead-making** (Lothal, Chanhudaro) | **Khambhat (Cambay)** bead industry survives; shell bangles in Bengal |
| Economy | World's earliest **cotton** cultivation and weaving | India's cotton-textile tradition |
| Agriculture | **Cross-ploughed field** (Kalibangan) | The same criss-cross furrow ploughing in NW India |
| Transport | **Bullock-cart** terracottas | Near-identical bullock carts in rural India |
| Civics | Standardised **weights**, grid streets, covered **drains** | Ideals of civic planning and sanitation; binary/decimal weight logic |

**The honest caveat (write this, examiners reward it):** continuity is a matter of **argument, not proof.** The **script is unread**, and surface resemblance (*post hoc, ergo propter hoc*) is not descent; several "continuities" — the yoga posture, the "proto-Śiva" reading — are **contested** (Doris Srinivasan and others doubt the Pashupati identification). The theme is also **politically loaded** (the "Sarasvatī / indigenous-continuity" school vs. the migrationists — §3.4). The Mains-safe verdict: *the Harappans did not vanish — Late-Harappan rural cultures (Cemetery H, Jhukar, Rangpur and the Chalcolithic successors) carried the population forward, and a recognisable substratum of ritual, craft and material life survives into the present — but each specific claim is a reasoned inference, never a certainty.*

**Answer hooks:**
- "**Transformation, not disappearance**": urban collapse ≠ cultural extinction; people and practice continued in the countryside.
- "**A living Bronze Age**": Śiva/Nandi, mother-goddess, pipal, swastika, sindūr, ritual bathing, cotton, Khambhat beads, cross-furrow ploughing — name three or four *concretely*.
- Always close on the **source caveat**: undeciphered script → continuity is inference, and the question is politicised.

## 3. Vedic Culture (c. 1500 – 600 BCE)

**The pivot:** after Harappan de-urbanisation, north India becomes, for a millennium, **rural, pastoral-then-agrarian, non-literate, and organised around Sanskrit oral texts and sacrifice**. The *only* substantial source is the **Vedic corpus itself** — so this is a period reconstructed from **literature, cross-checked against archaeology**.

### 3.1 The sources — and their bias

- **The four Vedas** — **Ṛgveda** (hymns; oldest, our window onto Early Vedic), **Sāmaveda** (melodies), **Yajurveda** (sacrificial formulae), **Atharvaveda** (spells, folk religion). Each Veda has **Saṃhitā → Brāhmaṇa (ritual prose) → Āraṇyaka → Upaniṣad (philosophy)**.
- **Preserved by oral transmission (śruti)** with astonishing fidelity, but **composed by and for a priestly elite** — so we see **ritual and the worldview of the varṇa order in high resolution, and the labouring majority barely at all**. Same donor-bias problem as later inscriptions.
- **Archaeological correlates:** **Painted Grey Ware (PGW)** culture (c. 1200–600 BCE, upper Ganga–Yamuna doab) is the material signature usually mapped onto the **Later Vedic** phase; the earlier phase is harder to pin archaeologically. **This text-to-pottery correlation is itself a debated inference — flag it.**

### 3.2 Early (Ṛgvedic) vs. Later Vedic — the transformation

This contrast is the **single most exam-worthy structure** in the section. Master the shift.

| Theme | **Early / Ṛgvedic** (c. 1500–1000 BCE) | **Later Vedic** (c. 1000–600 BCE) |
|---|---|---|
| **Geography** | **Sapta-Sindhu** — Punjab, the seven rivers, the Sarasvatī | Eastward shift to the **Ganga-Yamuna doab**, Kuru-Pañcāla heartland |
| **Economy** | **Pastoral**; cattle = wealth (*gau* = unit of value; *gaviṣṭi*, "search for cows," = war) | **Agrarian**; iron (*kṛṣṇa-ayas*) → plough → surplus; land gains value |
| **Polity** | **Tribal (jana)**; chief = *rājan*, checked by **Sabhā & Samiti**; no territory, no regular tax (*bali* = voluntary) | **Territorial (janapada)**; kingship larger, hereditary, ritually magnified (*rājasūya, aśvamedha, vājapeya*); Sabhā/Samiti decline; new officials (*ratnins*), *bali/bhāga* → regular dues |
| **Society** | Fluid; **varṇa emerging**; **Puruṣasūkta (RV 10.90)** — the **fourfold order** appears in the **latest** book | **Rigid varṇa**; Brahmin & Kshatriya supremacy entrenched; **untouchability germinating**; women's status **declining** |
| **Women** | Relatively better: education, remarriage, Sabhā presence; women seers (**Lopāmudrā, Ghoṣā, Apālā**) | Decline; excluded from assemblies and (increasingly) upanayana; subordination in Brāhmaṇa texts |
| **Religion** | **Nature gods** — **Indra** (war/rain, most hymns), **Agni** (fire, ~2nd), **Varuṇa** (cosmic order/ṛta), Soma; worship by **hymn & simple sacrifice**, no temples/images | **Ritualism triumphant** — **Prajāpati** rises; Viṣṇu & Rudra gain ground; **elaborate, costly, priest-dominated yajña**; the **Upaniṣadic reaction** begins (ātman-brahman, karma, mokṣa) |

### 3.3 The mechanisms to name (this is what turns facts into analysis)

- **Iron + the eastward move = the engine of the whole transformation.** Iron tools cleared the **wetter, forested doab**; **plough agriculture** produced **surplus**; surplus supported **larger kingship, a priestly class living off it, and social stratification**. **Pastoral tribe → agrarian proto-state.** *If you write one causal sentence in this section, write this one.*
- **Sacrifice as statecraft:** the great royal rituals (**aśvamedha, rājasūya**) are not mere religion — they are **legitimation technology**, converting a tribal chief into a territorial king and **redistributing/consuming surplus** while cementing the **Brahmin–Kshatriya alliance**.
- **Varṇa hardens from function to birth:** in the Ṛgveda status is fluid; by the Later Vedic Brāhmaṇas it is **hereditary, ritualised, and hierarchical** — the origin of the caste order and the **grievance base** that the heterodox sects (§5) would later mobilise.
- **The Upaniṣadic turn:** even *within* Vedic culture a reaction against costly ritualism begins — **knowledge (jñāna) over sacrifice (karma-kāṇḍa)**, the doctrines of **ātman, brahman, karma, saṃsāra, mokṣa**. This is the **internal bridge** to Jainism and Buddhism.

### 3.3a The institutions, the literature & the material record (details to deploy)

When a question wants substance beyond the Early-vs-Later contrast, reach for these:

- **The polity's vocabulary:** the tribal assemblies — **Sabhā** (a select council of elders/nobles), **Samiti** (the fuller assembly that could choose or approve the *rājan*), and the older, multi-functional **Vidatha** — plus the **gaṇa**. Officers were few: **purohita** (priest), **senānī** (war-leader), **grāmaṇī** (village head); **no bureaucracy, no standing army, no regular tax** in the Early Vedic. The Later Vedic adds the **ratnins** ("jewel-bearers," proto-ministers installed at the *rājasūya*) and grander titles (*samrāṭ, ekarāṭ*) — assemblies fade as kingship swells.
- **The literature, structured:** each Veda layers as **Saṃhitā → Brāhmaṇa → Āraṇyaka → Upaniṣad**; around them sit the **six Vedāṅgas** (Śikṣā, Kalpa, Vyākaraṇa, Nirukta, Chandas, Jyotiṣa) and the four **Upavedas** (Āyurveda, Dhanurveda, Gāndharvaveda, Śilpa/Artha). The philosophy lives in the **~108 Upaniṣads**; the **Gāyatrī mantra** (to Savitṛ) is Ṛgvedic.
- **Concepts to name (they crystallise in the Later Vedic–Sūtra age):** **ṛta** (cosmic order — the seed of dharma and karma), the **four puruṣārthas**, **four āśramas**, **three ṛṇas** (debts), **saṃskāras**, and **gotra**; **varṇa** beginning to fracture into **jāti**.
- **The material correlates — and their caveat:** **OCP (Ochre-Coloured Pottery)** and the **Copper Hoards** are sometimes tied to a Late-Harappan/early-Vedic horizon; **PGW** is mapped onto the Later Vedic doab. But the **text-to-pottery equation is inference, not proof** (§3.1) — say so.

### 3.4 The Aryan debate (handle with balance — examiners watch for objectivity)

- **"Aryan" is a linguistic/cultural category, not a race** — this is the essential opening line. It denotes speakers of an Indo-Aryan (Indo-European) language and the culture of the Vedas.
- **Two poles:** (a) **Migration/invasion** — Indo-Aryan speakers entered the north-west c. 2nd millennium BCE (linguistic kinship of Sanskrit with Avestan/Greek/Latin; horse & spoked-chariot; the **Boghazköy inscription** naming Vedic gods in Anatolia c. 1400 BCE). (b) **Indigenous (OIT)** — Aryans/Vedic culture are native, and the Harappans were Vedic (Sarasvatī prominence).
- **The measured position:** most scholarship favours **migration (a slow influx and admixture, not a single "invasion")**; **recent ancient-DNA work (incl. Rakhigarhi) supports steppe-related gene flow in the 2nd millennium BCE while also showing deep Harappan continuity.** The safe Mains stance: *reject the crude "invasion-and-massacre" model, acknowledge migration + admixture, and stress that the debate is unsettled and politically charged.*

### 3.5 Answer hooks

- "**Iron, the plough, and the move east** turned a cattle-herding tribe into a taxable agrarian kingdom" — the master sentence.
- "**Sacrifice was the state's software**": ritual legitimised kingship before administration could.
- "The Vedic age **manufactured the varṇa order** and, with it, the discontent that the sixth century BCE would unleash."
- Aryan question = **linguistic + archaeological + genetic** evidence, all cited, no ideology.

## 4. The Mahājanapadas & the Second Urbanisation (c. 600 BCE)

**What changes:** around the sixth century BCE north India is transformed again — **cities return** (the "**Second Urbanisation**," the first since Harappa), **coinage** (punch-marked silver *kārṣāpaṇas*) appears, **writing revives** (Brāhmī, attested surely from Ashoka but likely older), and the tribal *janapadas* consolidate into **16 large states, the Mahājanapadas.** This is the stage on which **Jainism, Buddhism, and Magadha** all rise.

### 4.1 The material base

- **Iron intensifies** in the middle-Ganga (associated with **Northern Black Polished Ware, NBPW**) → **surplus → cities → trade → coined money → a merchant class (śreṣṭhins, gahapatis)**. The **second urbanisation is agrarian-surplus + trade driven**, exactly as the first was.
- **New social forces:** wealthy merchants and a discontented **Vaiśya** class chafing under Brahmanical ritual monopoly; this economic rise **without matching ritual status** is a key driver of §5's religious revolt.
- **The material signature — read critically:** the fingerprints of the second urbanisation are **NBPW** (the lustrous "de-luxe" pottery), **burnt-brick houses, ring-wells and ramparts**, and the first Indian **coins — the silver-and-copper *punch-marked kārṣāpaṇas* (āhata)**, stamped by **guilds, merchants and states**. Source-caution to state: punch-marked coins carry **symbols, not legends**, so they are **hard to date or attribute to a named ruler** — they prove a **monetised, guild-organised trade economy** rather than any one king. **Writing revives** too (**Brāhmī**, and **Kharoṣṭhī** in the north-west) — securely attested from Aśoka but very likely **older**, its origin (Semitic-derived vs indigenous) still debated.

### 4.2 The sixteen Mahājanapadas

Listed in the **Buddhist *Aṅguttara Nikāya*** (and the Jaina *Bhagavatī Sūtra*). You need not recite all sixteen; **know the pattern and the key players.**

- **The sixteen (Aṅguttara list):** Kāsī, Kosala, **Aṅga**, **Magadha**, Vajji (Vṛji), Malla, Chedi, **Vatsa**, Kuru, Pañcāla, Matsya, Śūrasena, Assaka (the **only one south of the Vindhyas**, on the Godāvari), **Avanti**, Gandhāra, Kāmboja.
- **Two political forms — a crucial distinction:**
  - **Monarchies (rājya):** Magadha, Kosala, Vatsa, Avanti — the "**big four**" contenders for supremacy.
  - **Republics / oligarchies (gaṇa-saṅgha):** the **Vajji confederacy** (capital **Vaiśālī**, led by the **Licchavis**), the **Mallas**, **Śākyas** (the Buddha's clan), Koliyas. Governed by **assemblies of the Kshatriya elite**, not a single king — an early, much-cited experiment in **collective/oligarchic governance**. *(Do not romanticise them as democracies — franchise was confined to a Kshatriya aristocracy.)*

**The sixteen at a glance (capital · region · form):**

| Mahājanapada | Capital | Region (modern) | Form |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Magadha** | Girivraja/Rājagṛha → Pāṭaliputra | S. Bihar | Monarchy — *the eventual victor* |
| **Kosala** | Śrāvastī (Ayodhyā/Sāketa) | E. Uttar Pradesh | Monarchy |
| **Vatsa** | Kauśāmbī | Around Prayāgrāj, UP | Monarchy |
| **Avanti** | Ujjayinī (N.) / Māhiṣmatī (S.) | Malwa, MP | Monarchy — *the great western rival* |

| **Aṅga** | Champā | E. Bihar | Monarchy *(absorbed early by Magadha)* |
| **Vajji (Vṛji)** | Vaiśālī | N. Bihar | **Republic (confederacy, Licchavis)** |
| **Malla** | Kuśīnagara / Pāvā | E. UP | **Republic** |
| **Chedi** | Śuktimatī | Bundelkhand, MP | Monarchy |

### 4.2a The gaṇa-saṅghas — India's early experiment in collective rule

The republics are the section's analytical prize; know how they actually worked and why they lost:

- **How they governed:** sovereignty lay not in one king but in an **assembly (santhāgāra)** of the **Kṣatriya heads of families (each a "rājā")**, who met, debated, and **decided by vote** — the Buddhist texts even describe the procedure (motions *ñatti*, tellers/ballot-collectors *śalākā-gāhāpaka*, quorum rules) — with an elected **chief (gaṇa-mukhya / senāpati)** presiding. The **Licchavi-led Vajji confederacy** is the model; the Buddha admired its concord and taught the Vajjis the **seven aparihāniya-dhammas** ("conditions of no-decline").
- **The limit — say it to dodge the "ancient democracy" trap:** the franchise was an **aristocratic oligarchy of the Kṣatriya elite**; **women, śūdras, dāsas and the labouring majority were excluded.** These were **oligarchic republics, not democracies.**
- **Why they lost to the monarchies:** **internal factionalism** (Ajātaśatru's minister **Vassakāra broke the Licchavis by sowing discord** — statecraft, not the sword), the **absence of unified command**, and the greater **fiscal-military concentration** a centralised monarchy could muster (§6). Surviving pockets are still listed by Kauṭilya and were finally crushed under **Samudragupta**.
- **Why they still matter:** they are India's indigenous tradition of **non-monarchical, deliberative government** — a genuine, if aristocratic, road-not-taken that the territorial monarchies extinguished (rehabilitated for modern readers by **K.P. Jayaswal's *Hindu Polity***).

### 4.3 The struggle for supremacy — and why Magadha won

The **big four** fought for a century; **Magadha absorbed the rest** (see §6). The mechanisms of Magadha's victory (marshalled in full there) are the payoff of this section.

### 4.4 Answer hooks

- **Second urbanisation** = cities + coins + writing + iron-surplus, **the material stage for the religious and imperial revolutions**.
- **Monarchy vs. gaṇa-saṅgha** is the structural contrast to sell; Vajji/Licchavi = the celebrated republican model.
- Economic rise of merchants **without ritual status** = the socio-economic fuel of Jainism & Buddhism.

## 5. The Emergence of New Religions — Jainism & Buddhism

**Framing:** the sixth century BCE saw a **subcontinent-wide intellectual ferment** — Buddhist texts count **62 (or "63") heterodox schools**, including the **Ājīvikas (Makkhali Gosāla, fatalism)** and **Cārvāka/Lokāyata (materialism)**. **Jainism and Buddhism are the two that survived and reshaped India.** They are best understood as a **response to a specific crisis**, not as inexplicable spiritual novelties.

### 5.1 Why *then*? — the causes (this is the analytical heart)

1. **Religious:** revolt against **costly, priest-monopolised Vedic ritualism** and the incomprehension of Sanskrit; the **Upaniṣadic turn** had already primed an inward, ethical, salvation-seeking mood.
2. **Social:** rejection of the **rigid varṇa order** and Brahmin supremacy. Note both founders were **Kshatriyas** — a Kshatriya assertion against Brahmin ritual dominance.
3. **Economic:** the **rising merchant/gahapati class** wanted a religion that (a) gave them **dignity** the varṇa order denied, and (b) **sanctioned trade and accumulation** — Vedic ritual demanded wasteful **animal sacrifice** (killing the cattle that were now agrarian capital). **Ahiṃsā protected the plough-ox; frugality suited the merchant.** Both faiths won **mercantile patronage** precisely here.
4. **Economic (agrarian):** **ahiṃsā and opposition to cattle-slaughter** protected the animals essential to the new **plough-and-cart** economy.

### 5.2 Jainism

- **The Tīrthaṅkara scheme:** Jainism claims **24 Tīrthaṅkaras** ("ford-makers"). The **first is Ṛṣabhanātha/Ādinātha**; the **23rd, Pārśvanātha** (c. 8th c. BCE, historical, taught 4 vows); the **24th, Vardhamāna Mahāvīra** (c. 599–527 BCE), the great reformer — *not* the founder, a point examiners like.
- **Mahāvīra:** born **Kuṇḍagrāma (near Vaiśālī)** to a Kshatriya (Jñātṛka clan) family; renounced at 30; attained **kaivalya** (omniscience) after 12 years; titles **Jina** ("conqueror") and **Mahāvīra**; died/**attained nirvāṇa at Pāvāpurī**.
- **Doctrine:** the goal is **liberation of the soul (jīva)** from **karma (subtle matter)** clinging to it. Path = the **Three Jewels (Triratna): Right Faith, Right Knowledge, Right Conduct.** Conduct = the **five vows**: **ahiṃsā, satya, asteya (non-stealing), aparigraha (non-possession), brahmacarya** (the fifth added by Mahāvīra to Pārśva's four). Epistemology: **anekāntavāda** (many-sidedness) & **syādvāda** (conditional predication). **Extreme asceticism**, even **sallekhanā** (ritual fasting to death). **No creator-God**; the universe is eternal; **karma is impersonal and mechanical**.
- **The schism:** at the **Council of Pāṭaliputra** (c. 3rd c. BCE) and finally the **Council of Vallabhī (5th c. CE, canon compiled in Ardhamāgadhī)**, Jainism split into **Digambara ("sky-clad," nudity, southern, ascetic)** and **Śvetāmbara ("white-clad," northern)** — over nudity, women's capacity for liberation, and canon.
- **Spread & patrons:** **Candragupta Maurya** (tradition: migrated south with **Bhadrabāhu** to **Śravaṇabelagoḷa** and died by sallekhanā), **Kharavela of Kalinga**, western Indian merchants (hence its survival in **Gujarat, Rajasthan, Karnataka**). **Contribution:** **Prakrit/Apabhraṃśa and regional languages** (Jain scholars enriched **Kannada, Tamil, Gujarati**), temple architecture (**Dilwara**, Śravaṇabelagoḷa's **Gomaṭeśvara**), and an ethic of **non-violence and vegetarianism**.

### 5.3 Buddhism

- **The Buddha (c. 563–483 BCE; some place c. 480–400):** born **Siddhārtha Gautama** at **Lumbinī** to the **Śākya** (a gaṇa-saṅgha) chief Śuddhodana; the **Four Sights** (old man, sick man, corpse, ascetic) → the **Great Renunciation (mahābhiniṣkramaṇa)**; **enlightenment (bodhi) at Bodh Gayā** under the pipal; **first sermon (Dharmacakra-pravartana) at Sārnāth**; **mahāparinirvāṇa at Kuśīnagara**. The **four great sites** (Lumbinī, Bodh Gayā, Sārnāth, Kuśīnagara) are standard.
- **The teaching — the "middle path" between indulgence and extreme asceticism** (his implicit critique of Jain-style mortification):
  - **Four Noble Truths:** (1) life is suffering (*duḥkha*); (2) suffering is caused by desire/craving (*tṛṣṇā*); (3) suffering can cease (*nirvāṇa*); (4) the way is the **Eightfold Path**.
  - **Eightfold Path (*aṣṭāṅgika mārga*):** right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness, concentration.
  - **Core philosophy:** **anātman** (no permanent soul — *contrast with Jainism's eternal jīva*), **anitya** (impermanence), **pratītyasamutpāda** (dependent origination), **karma & rebirth**, **nirvāṇa** as the extinction of craving. **God is irrelevant/silent; the emphasis is ethical and psychological.** Vehicle: the **Saṅgha** (monastic order, open across caste — its social radicalism).
- **The Councils** (learn the four — a favourite):

| Council | Date | Place | Patron | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **First** | c. 483 BCE | Rājagṛha | Ajātaśatru | Compiled the Buddha's teachings — **Sutta** (Ānanda) & **Vinaya** (Upāli) piṭakas |
| **Second** | c. 383 BCE | Vaiśālī | Kālāśoka | Dispute over monastic rules → first schism (Sthaviravāda vs. Mahāsāṅghika) |
| **Third** | c. 250 BCE | Pāṭaliputra | **Ashoka** | Canon finalised (**Abhidhamma** added → **Tripiṭaka**); **Moggaliputta Tissa**; missions dispatched (incl. Mahinda/Saṅghamitrā to Sri Lanka) |
| **Fourth** | c. 1st c. CE | Kashmir (Kuṇḍalavana) | **Kaniṣka** | **Vasumitra/Aśvaghoṣa**; texts in **Sanskrit**; the **Hīnayāna–Mahāyāna** split crystallises |

- **The great schism — Hīnayāna vs. Mahāyāna** (post-Kaniṣka): **Theravāda/Hīnayāna** ("lesser vehicle," self-liberation, Pali, no idol-worship, historical Buddha; survives in Sri Lanka, SE Asia) vs. **Mahāyāna** ("greater vehicle," **Bodhisattva** ideal of universal salvation, **Buddha-worship with images**, Sanskrit; spreads to China, Japan, Tibet, Central Asia). **Vajrayāna** (tantric) emerges later.
- **Spread & decline:** rapid spread via **royal patronage (Ashoka, Kaniṣka)**, the **Saṅgha, Prakrit/vernacular preaching, and trade routes**. **Decline in India** (post-Gupta, gone by c. 12th c.) through **loss of patronage, revived & reformed Hinduism (Bhakti, Śaṅkara), corruption/ritualisation of the Saṅgha (Tantra), and the destruction of monasteries (Nālandā, Vikramaśīla) in the Turkish invasions.** *Its ideas survived by absorption into Hinduism (Buddha as an avatāra of Viṣṇu).*

### 5.3a Sacred canon & the philosophical schools (the deeper cut)

*For the questions that ask for doctrine and literature, not just causes.*

**Jainism — canon & metaphysics.**
- **Canon:** the **Āgamas** — traditionally **12 Aṅgas** (the 12th, *Dṛṣṭivāda*, with the ancient *Pūrvas*, is lost) plus Upāṅgas, finally compiled at the **Council of Vallabhī (c. 5th c. CE)** in **Ardhamāgadhī Prākrit**. **Digambaras hold the original canon irretrievably lost** — itself part of the sectarian rift.
- **Metaphysics — realist and pluralist:** reality is **jīva (soul)** and **ajīva (non-soul)**; ajīva comprises **pudgala (matter), dharma & adharma (the media of motion and rest), ākāśa (space)** and **kāla (time)** — the **six dravyas**. **Karma is literally subtle matter** that clings to and weighs down the soul: **bondage (bandha) → stoppage (saṃvara) → shedding (nirjarā) → liberation (mokṣa)**.
- **Logic — the Jain signature:** **anekāntavāda** (reality is many-sided), worked out as **nayavāda** (standpoints) and **syādvāda / saptabhaṅgī** (the seven-fold conditional "maybe" predication) — a distinctive contribution to Indian epistemology.

**Buddhism — canon & the philosophical schools.**
- **Canon — the Tripiṭaka (Pali):** **Sutta** (discourses), **Vinaya** (monastic rule), **Abhidhamma** (scholastic analysis); plus non-canonical gems — the ***Milindapañha***, the Sri Lankan chronicles (***Dīpavaṃsa, Mahāvaṃsa***), and above all the **Jātakas** (birth-stories: priceless social history and the seedbed of early Buddhist art).
- **The four schools (a favourite):** **Vaibhāṣika** and **Sautrāntika** — the two **Hīnayāna realist** schools (direct vs inferred perception of external objects); **Yogācāra / Vijñānavāda** (**Asaṅga, Vasubandhu** — "mind-only" idealism); and **Mādhyamika** (**Nāgārjuna** — **śūnyatā**, emptiness/relativity, the true "middle"). The last two are the **Mahāyāna** philosophies; **Vajrayāna** later adds tantra, mantra and maṇḍala.

**Contribution to Indian civilisation (both faiths):** **vernacular scripture** (Pali, Ardhamāgadhī, Apabhraṃśa); **logic and dialectic**; the **monastic universities** (Nālandā, Valabhī, Vikramaśīla); **art and architecture** (stūpa, caitya, the first Buddha images — §7A, §9.3); **ahiṃsā and vegetarianism** as pan-Indian ethics; and a **caste-critical, egalitarian** social impulse whose memory outlived the institutions.

### 5.4 Jainism vs. Buddhism — the comparison (examiners love this)

| Axis | **Jainism** | **Buddhism** |
|---|---|---|
| Soul | **Eternal jīva** in everything | **Anātman** — no permanent soul |
| Ahiṃsā | **Extreme/absolute** (even microbes) | **Moderate**, the Middle Path |
| Asceticism | Severe (sallekhanā) | Rejected extremes — Middle Path |
| God | No creator; universe eternal | Silent on God; ethical focus |
| Language | **Prakrit (Ardhamāgadhī)** | **Pali** (later Sanskrit) |
| Spread | Stayed **within India** (Gujarat, Raj., Karnataka) | Became a **world religion** (Asia-wide) |
| Survival | **Survives in India** | **Declined in India**, thrives abroad |

### 5.5 Answer hooks

- Both are **socio-economic responses** to the second urbanisation, not free-floating spiritual events: **merchant dignity + ahiṃsā protecting agrarian capital + Kshatriya revolt against Brahmin ritual.**
- **Ahiṃsā had an economic logic** — end wasteful cattle-sacrifice, protect the plough-and-cart economy.
- The **Saṅgha's caste-blind entry** was Buddhism's social dynamite; **vernacular preaching (Pali/Prakrit)** its reach.
- **Buddhism became global, Jainism stayed Indian but endured** — explain via missionary universalism vs. rigorous asceticism.

## 6. The Rise of Magadha & the Age of the Mauryas

### 6.1 Why Magadha rose — the causes of its supremacy (a set-piece answer)

Magadha (capitals **Rājagṛha → Pāṭaliputra**) beat Kosala, Vatsa and Avanti through a **convergence of geography, resources and ambition**:

1. **Geography/defensibility:** early capital **Rājagṛha ringed by five hills**; later **Pāṭaliputra** at the **Ganga-Son-Gandak-Ghaghara confluence** — a **water-fort** commanding river traffic and trade tolls.
2. **Iron:** proximity to the rich **iron mines of south Bihar (around Rājgir/Dhalbhum)** → superior **weapons and tools**.
3. **Agriculture:** the **fertile middle-Ganga** with heavy rainfall → large **surplus** to feed armies.
4. **The elephant:** eastern forests gave Magadha **war-elephants** the western states lacked — a decisive military asset later noted by the Greeks.
5. **Trade & river control** over the Ganga = revenue.
6. **Ambitious rulers** who were **unscrupulous and expansionist** (see below).

### 6.2 The pre-Mauryan dynasties (the run-up)

- **Haryaṅka:** **Bimbisāra** (c. 544–492 BCE) — began expansion by **conquest (Aṅga) and matrimonial alliances** (Kosala, Licchavi, Madra); contemporary of the Buddha & Mahāvīra. His son **Ajātaśatru** (492–460) — annexed **Kāśī and the Vajji/Licchavi confederacy** (using new war-engines, the *rathamusala* and *mahāśilākaṇṭaka*), fortified **Pāṭaligrāma**. **Udāyin** founded **Pāṭaliputra**.
- **Śiśunāga:** temporarily crushed the long rival **Avanti** — removing Magadha's last great competitor.
- **Nanda:** **Mahāpadma Nanda** — the "**first great historical empire-builder**," a low-born (Śūdra) usurper who styled himself *Ekarāt/Sarva-kṣatrāntaka*; amassed a **vast army and treasury**. The **Nandas' wealth and standing army** are what the Greeks (Alexander's companions) heard of as the "Gangaridai/Prasii." **Dhana Nanda's** unpopularity and Alexander's presence set the stage for **Candragupta**.

### 6.3 The Mauryan Empire (c. 321 – 185 BCE) — India's first pan-Indian state

**Founding:** **Candragupta Maurya**, aided by **Kauṭilya/Cāṇakya/Viṣṇugupta**, **overthrew the Nandas (c. 321 BCE)**. He then **defeated Seleucus Nikator (c. 305 BCE)**, gaining the **trans-Indus provinces (Arachosia, Aria, Gedrosia, Paropanisadae)** in exchange for **500 war-elephants** and a marriage alliance; Seleucus sent **Megasthenes** as ambassador (author of the *Indica*, our great Greek source). Candragupta's empire ran from Afghanistan to the Deccan — the **first unification of most of the subcontinent.** Tradition: he became a **Jain**, abdicated, and died by **sallekhanā at Śravaṇabelagoḷa**.

- **Bindusāra** (c. 297–273 BCE), "**Amitraghāta**" (slayer of foes), extended control into the **Deccan** (down to Karnataka/Andhra frontier); linked to the **Ājīvikas**; corresponded with Antiochus I (asked, per Greek accounts, for wine, figs and a Sophist).
- **Ashoka** (c. 268–232 BCE) — see §7.
- **Decline (after Ashoka):** a rapid slide over ~50 years to **185 BCE**, when the last Maurya **Bṛhadratha was assassinated by his general Puṣyamitra Śuṅga**. Causes to marshal: **weak successors & partition** of the empire; a **top-heavy, over-centralised bureaucracy** dependent on the ruler's personality; an **immense financial burden** (huge army + administration); **Brahmanical reaction** to Ashoka's pro-Buddhist, anti-sacrifice policy (Haraprasad Sastri's thesis — cite but qualify); **fiscal/monetary strain** (debased silver punch-marks); **provincial oppression & revolt**; and **north-western exposure** to invasions the weakened centre couldn't repel. **Best line: multi-causal — a personality-dependent centralised empire could not survive weak successors.**

### 6.4 Mauryan administration — the Arthaśāstra state (a favourite Mains topic)

The Mauryas built India's **first elaborate centralised bureaucracy**, reconstructable from **Kauṭilya's *Arthaśāstra*** (a treatise on statecraft — *prescriptive*, so read with care), **Megasthenes' *Indica*** (external, partly garbled), and **Ashoka's inscriptions** (the only *contemporary official* source).

- **Highly centralised monarchy** advised by a **mantri-pariṣad**; the king the pivot of all authority (**paternal despotism** — Ashoka: "*all men are my children*").
- **Provinces (c. 4–5)** under **Kumāras (royal princes)** — **Taxila** (north-west), **Ujjain** (west), **Suvarṇagiri** (south, Deccan), **Tosali** (Kalinga), with the centre at **Pāṭaliputra**.
- **A vast officialdom:** *amātyas, mahāmātras* (incl. Ashoka's **Dhamma-mahāmātras**), *adhyakṣas* (superintendents of trade, agriculture, mines, weaving, etc. — a near-**command economy**), *yuktas, rajukas, pradeśikas* (revenue/justice at district level), *gopas* and *sthānikas* (locally). An extensive **espionage system (gūḍha-puruṣas)**.
- **Megasthenes' city administration of Pāṭaliputra:** government by **six committees of five (30 officials)** overseeing crafts, foreigners, births/deaths, trade, manufactures, and sales-tax. His "**seven castes**" of Indian society is a **famous misreading** (he saw occupational groups as endogamous castes) — cite it as a caution on foreign sources.
- **Economy:** **land revenue (bhāga, ~1/4–1/6) the mainstay**; **state monopolies** (mines, salt, liquor, arms); **state farms (sītā lands)**; irrigation (the **Sudarśana Lake at Girnar**, built by Candragupta's governor Puṣyagupta, repaired under Rudradāman — a rare thread across dynasties); a regulated, **taxed and inspected** commercial economy.
- **Army:** Megasthenes/Pliny report a colossal force (600,000 foot, 30,000 horse, 9,000 elephants under the Nandas/Mauryas) run by a **war-office of six boards of five**.

### 6.5 Answer hooks

- Magadha's rise = **iron + elephants + fertile Ganga + defensible river-forts + ruthless kings** — the classic five-point answer.
- The Mauryan state is **India's first attempt at centralised, bureaucratic, near-command governance** — but read the *Arthaśāstra* as **an ideal, cross-checked by Megasthenes (external) and Ashoka (contemporary)**.
- Decline = **multi-causal**, but the deepest structural flaw was **over-centralisation dependent on a strong personality**.

## 7. Ashoka & His Dhamma

**Why Ashoka matters uniquely:** he is the **first Indian ruler we can hear in his own voice**, through the **Edicts** — the earliest surviving, dated, **decipherable** royal inscriptions in India (read by **James Prinsep, 1837**). Everything about him is therefore unusually **evidenced, not inferred.**

### 7.1 The turning point — Kalinga (c. 261 BCE)

- **Rock Edict XIII** — Ashoka's own, extraordinary confession: the **Kalinga war** left **100,000 slain, 150,000 deported, many times that dead of its aftermath**; the king felt **remorse (anuśocanā)** and turned from **Digvijaya (conquest by arms) to Dhamma-vijaya (conquest by righteousness).** This is the **single most-cited passage** in ancient Indian history.
- His conversion to **Buddhism** (as a **lay follower, upāsaka**, deepening over time — attested by the **Bhabru/Bairat edict** where he addresses the Saṅgha) followed. **But note:** *Ashoka the ruler propagated Dhamma, not sectarian Buddhism* — a crucial distinction (below).

### 7.2 The Edicts — the source base (know the categories)

- **14 Major Rock Edicts** (at frontier/peninsular sites — Girnar, Dhauli, Jaugada, Shahbazgarhi, Kalsi, Sopara, Yerragudi…), **Minor Rock Edicts**, **7 Pillar Edicts** (Ganga heartland — the polished **Mauryan monolithic pillars**, e.g. Sārnāth), and special edicts (the **two Kalinga/Separate edicts** at Dhauli & Jaugada; the **Rummindei/Lumbinī pillar** recording his pilgrimage & tax-remission).
- **Languages & scripts:** mostly **Prakrit in Brāhmī**; **Kharoṣṭhī** in the north-west; and, at **Shahbazgarhi/Mansehra** and above all the **Kandahar (Afghanistan) edicts**, **Greek and Aramaic** — proof he addressed subjects in their own tongues. Ashoka calls himself **"Devānāṃpiya Piyadasi"** ("Beloved-of-the-Gods, of gracious mien"); the **Maski & Gujarra minor edicts** clinched his identity by naming "**Asoka**."

### 7.3 What *was* Dhamma? — the analytical core

Dhamma was **not a religion** and **not merely Buddhism**. It was a **practical, ethical, non-sectarian code of civic conduct and social responsibility** designed to hold a diverse empire together. Its contents (from the edicts):

- **Ahiṃsā / non-violence** and restraint on animal slaughter (RE I; regulation of the royal kitchen and hunts).
- **Tolerance and respect for all sects (RE XII)** — "*honour another's sect, and you exalt your own*"; concord (*samavāya*) among religions.
- **Respect to elders, teachers, Brahmins & śramaṇas; kindness to servants, slaves, the poor; generosity (dāna); truthfulness.**
- **Welfare measures (RE II):** medical care for men and animals, wells, rest-houses, roadside trees, cultivation of medicinal herbs — a **paternal welfare state** ("*all men are my children*," Separate Edicts).
- **Administrative machinery of Dhamma:** appointment of **Dhamma-mahāmātras** (officers of morality/welfare, RE V), **Dhamma-yātrās** (moral tours replacing pleasure/hunting tours), inscribing edicts, and instructing officials to promote it.

**The essential exam distinction:** *Ashoka's personal faith was Buddhism; his state policy, Dhamma, was a deliberately ecumenical civic ethic* — which is exactly why it could bind a multi-religious empire and why calling him a mere Buddhist proselytiser is wrong.

### 7.4 Assessment — greatness, and the critique (give both)

- **The case for greatness:** the transformation of **kingship into ethical, paternal, welfare-oriented rule**; **religious tolerance** as royal policy (astonishing for the ancient world); the **spread of Buddhism to a world religion** (missions to Sri Lanka — Mahinda/Saṅghamitrā — Central Asia, the Hellenistic west); a lasting **imperial ideal**; and — via the **Sārnāth lion capital** and the **Dharmacakra** — the **symbols of the modern Indian republic**. H.G. Wells' famous encomium is quotable.
- **The critique (for balance):** did **Dhamma / pacifism weaken the empire's military fibre** and hasten Mauryan decline? (An old thesis — Ashoka himself **retained the army and the death penalty**, and RE XIII **warns the forest tribes he can still punish**, so the "pacifist who disarmed the state" charge is **overstated**.) Did the **anti-sacrifice stance provoke Brahmanical backlash** (Puṣyamitra)? (Plausible but not proven.) The measured verdict: *a visionary experiment in ethical governance whose decline owed more to structural over-centralisation and weak successors than to Dhamma itself.*

### 7.5 Answer hooks

- **Kalinga (RE XIII) = Digvijaya → Dhamma-vijaya**: the hinge, in his own words.
- **Dhamma ≠ Buddhism**: a **non-sectarian civic-ethical code** to integrate a plural empire (RE XII tolerance; RE II welfare; RE V Dhamma-mahāmātras).
- Ashoka is **the first Indian ruler in his own voice** (Prinsep, 1837; Brāhmī/Kharoṣṭhī/Greek/Aramaic).
- Assessment: **balance the visionary welfare-king against the "pacifism-weakened-Magadha" critique** — and conclude the critique is overstated.

## 7A. Mauryan Art & Architecture — court art and the popular tradition

**Why it is tested:** the Mauryan age is the first great horizon of Indian art in **stone**, and the standard question is a **contrast** — the **courtly, imperial, "foreign-touched" art the state patronised** versus the **indigenous "popular" tradition** of yakṣas, potters and craftsmen. Master that binary and the answer writes itself. (The evidence divides on the §0 rule: the pillars are read through Aśoka's *inscriptions*; the folk sculpture through *archaeology*.)

### 7A.1 Court art — the imperial, state-sponsored tradition

- **The monolithic pillars (stambhas).** Around twenty survive: free-standing and **non-structural**, each a single shaft of polished **Chunar sandstone**, up to ~15 m and ~50 tonnes, crowned by an **inverted-lotus (bell/"Persepolitan") capital → abacus → animal**. The **Sārnāth Lion Capital** — four addorsed lions over an abacus bearing lion, bull, horse and elephant with four small *dharma-cakras* — is **independent India's State Emblem**, and its wheel the **Aśoka Cakra** of the flag. Others: the **Rāmpūrvā bull**, the **Sāṅkiśya elephant**, the **Lauriyā-Nandangarh lion**.
- **The "Mauryan polish."** A glassy, mirror-like burnish on hard sandstone — a **technological fingerprint** so distinctive it is used to *date* objects as Mauryan.
- **Palace at Pāṭaliputra.** The **eighty-pillared hypostyle hall** (Kumrahar) astonished **Megasthenes** and, centuries later, **Fa-Hien**, who thought it the work of **spirits, not men**; the timber palisade (Bulandibagh) confirms the wooden city the Greeks described.
- **Rock-cut architecture begins here.** The **Barābar and Nāgārjunī caves** near Gayā are the **earliest surviving rock-cut caves in India**, dedicated by **Aśoka and Daśaratha to the Ājīvikas** — a striking datum: the Buddhist emperor endowing a *rival* sect, his ecumenism cut into granite. The **Lomas Ṛṣi** façade imitates a **wooden caitya arch** — ancestor of the whole caitya-cave tradition (Bhājā, Kārle, Ajanta).

### 7A.2 The foreign-influence debate (Persepolitan/Hellenistic vs indigenous)

- **The case for borrowing:** the **bell-shaped lotus capital** and the **polish** recall **Achaemenid Persepolis** — whose columned halls fell to Alexander just decades earlier, scattering craftsmen eastward — and the animal realism echoes Hellenistic form.
- **The case for indigeneity:** the pillars are **monolithic** (Persian ones are built up in drums) and **free-standing** (Persian ones carry roofs), and the **animals, lotus and cakra are wholly Indian symbols**; **Niharranjan Ray** and others read the whole as an **Indian achievement using a shared West-Asian visual vocabulary**.
- **The Mains-safe verdict:** *form and technique show contact with the Achaemenid-Hellenistic world (natural, given §8.1), but conception, symbolism and execution are Indian — borrowing of idiom, not of meaning.*

### 7A.3 Popular art — the non-court tradition

- **Yakṣa & Yakṣī sculpture:** colossal, free-standing **folk deities** — the polished **Didarganj Yakṣī (chāmara-bearer)**, the **Parkham Yakṣa**, the Patna yakṣas — massive, frontal, worldly, rooted in **popular fertility cults**, not the court (some scholars date Didarganj to Śuṅga/Kushāṇa — flag it).
- **Terracottas, pottery, coins:** mass **terracotta figurines**; the fine, lustrous **NBPW**, the type-fossil of the age; and the **punch-marked coins** of §4.
- **Stūpas:** the Mauryas (Aśoka, traditionally **84,000 stūpas**) inaugurated stūpa-building; the **core of Sāñchī** is Mauryan — but its famous carved **toraṇa gateways are later Śuṅga-Sātavāhana work** (*foundation ≠ fabric*).

### 7A.4 Answer hooks

- **Court vs popular** is the organising contrast: **pillars, capitals, palace, Barābar caves (court)** vs **yakṣa/yakṣī, terracotta, folk cults (popular)**.
- **Sārnāth capital = the Republic's emblem**; the **polish** is the Mauryan signature.
- **Barābar = India's first rock-cut caves, Ājīvika-dedicated** — art as Aśokan ecumenism.
- Foreign influence: **idiom borrowed, meaning Indian** — never concede more.

## 8. Foreign Invasions on India (the North-West Corridor)

**The pattern to grasp:** India's **north-west (the passes — Khyber, Bolan)** is the **permanent invasion corridor**. From the sixth century BCE onward a succession of powers — **Persians, Greeks (Alexander), then, after the Mauryan collapse, Indo-Greeks, Śakas, Parthians (Pahlavas), and Kushāṇas** — pour through it. The story is **less about conquest than about cultural fusion**: the north-west becomes ancient India's **cosmopolitan crossroads**.

### 8.1 The Achaemenid Persians (c. 6th–4th c. BCE)

- **Cyrus** and then **Darius I** (c. 518 BCE) annexed the Indus region; the **"Hi(n)dush" satrapy** was the **most populous and richest** of the Persian empire (per Herodotus, paying huge gold tribute). **Impact:** introduced **Kharoṣṭhī script (Aramaic-derived)**, spurred **Indo-Persian trade and administrative ideas**, and possibly influenced **Mauryan pillar art** (the Persepolitan polish and bell-capitals). It also **opened the door of India's north-west to the West**, informing the Greeks of India's wealth.

### 8.2 Alexander of Macedon (327–325 BCE)

- Having crushed the Persians, **Alexander** crossed the Indus (327/326 BCE). **Ambhi (Āmbhi) of Taxila submitted**; the great battle was against **Porus (Puru) on the Hydaspes (Jhelum, 326 BCE)** — Alexander won but, impressed, reinstated Porus. His army, exhausted and afraid of the **Nanda power beyond the Beas (Hyphasis)**, **mutinied**; he turned back and **died at Babylon (323 BCE)**.
- **Assessment — militarily marginal, historically catalytic:** he **held nothing** (his Indian conquests evaporated within a decade, absorbed by Candragupta). But he (a) **exposed the political fragmentation** of the north-west and the weakness of the Nandas — arguably **easing Candragupta's unification**; (b) opened **land and sea routes** and durable **Indo-Greek contact**; (c) his companions' accounts give us our **first firm chronological anchor** ("**the sheet-anchor of ancient Indian chronology**") and priceless descriptions; (d) seeded the **Indo-Greek states** and, later, **Gandhāra art**. *The paradox to sell: a failed conquest with vast indirect consequences.*

### 8.3 The post-Mauryan invaders (c. 2nd c. BCE – 1st c. CE)

The Mauryan collapse (185 BCE) reopened the north-west to a **relay of Central-Asian peoples**, each pushed by the one behind it (the ultimate driver being the movement of the **Yuezhi/Xiongnu** in Central Asia):

- **Indo-Greeks / Bactrian Greeks (c. 2nd c. BCE):** the **first** to invade post-Maurya. **Demetrius** and above all **Menander (Milinda)** — whose dialogue with the monk Nāgasena, the **Milindapañha**, records his interest in Buddhism. **Contributions:** the **first gold coins** and the **first coins bearing king-portraits and legends (bilingual, dated)** in India — the beginning of **numismatics as history**; and the **Gandhāra school of art** (Greco-Buddhist).
- **Śakas (Scythians, c. 1st c. BCE):** displaced the Indo-Greeks; ruled in several branches (the **Western Kṣatrapas** of Ujjain the most durable). **Rudradāman I** (c. 150 CE) — his **Junāgaḍh/Girnar rock inscription** (repair of the **Sudarśana Lake**) is the **first long inscription in polished Sanskrit** (a landmark in the shift from Prakrit to Sanskrit epigraphy). The **Śaka era (78 CE)** — India's national calendar — is conventionally linked to this milieu (often to Kaniṣka; see below). **Vikramāditya of Ujjain** is remembered for expelling Śakas (the legendary basis of the **Vikrama Saṃvat, 58 BCE**).
- **Parthians / Pahlavas (c. 1st c. CE):** briefly ruled the north-west; **Gondophernes** is associated (in Christian tradition) with the visit of **St. Thomas the Apostle** to India.
- **Kushāṇas (Yuezhi):** the greatest of these — treated fully in §9.

### 8.4 Answer hooks

- The **north-west corridor** is the structural constant; the story is **cultural fusion**, not just conquest.
- **Persians** → Kharoṣṭhī + possible pillar-art influence + opened India to the West.
- **Alexander** = **militarily trivial, historically catalytic** — chronology-anchor, routes, Indo-Greek legacy, eased Mauryan unification. Sell the paradox.
- **The invader-relay** (Greek → Śaka → Pahlava → Kushāṇa) is driven by a **Central-Asian domino** and leaves India **coins (Indo-Greeks), Sanskrit epigraphy (Rudradāman), and Gandhāra art**.

## 8A. The Deccan in the Same Centuries — the Sātavāhanas (the first great Āndhra power)

> **Scope & why this section exists.** While the north-west absorbed the foreign relay (§8), the **Deccan threw up the first great indigenous empire of post-Mauryan India — the Sātavāhanas (c. 230 BCE – c. 220 CE)** — contemporaries and rivals of the Śakas, and near-contemporaries of the Kushāṇas (§9), with whom they shared the Roman trade. They belong here because they (a) complete the "post-Mauryan India" picture the exam expects, and (b) are **the foundation of Andhra history** — for **APPSC the single most important ancient dynasty**, and the natural prelude to the Ikṣvāku–Viṣṇukuṇḍin–Eastern-Chāḷukya story of the companion Telugu-country notes. *Expect this unit to be tested more heavily in APPSC than the pan-Indian syllabus alone would suggest.*

### 8A.1 Origins & the "who were they?" debate

- The **Purāṇas** call them **Āndhras** (or **Āndhra-bhṛtyas**, "servants of the Andhras"); their **own inscriptions** call them **Sātavāhanas**. **Founder: Simuka** (c. 230 BCE), rising as Mauryan authority collapsed.
- **The debates (flag, don't resolve):**
  - *Āndhra vs Sātavāhana* — is "Āndhra" the ethnic/regional name and "Sātavāhana" the dynastic one?
  - *Original homeland* — **western Deccan** (Maharashtra, around **Pratiṣṭhāna / Paithan**) or the **eastern Andhra** Krishna–Godāvarī country? The **earliest inscriptions (Nāneghāṭ, Nāsik) are western**, so most hold they **rose in the west and expanded east** — though the Purāṇic "Āndhra" label and the later eastern capitals pull the other way.
  - *Varṇa* — Gautamī Balaśrī's praśasti claims Brahmin status (*eka-bamhaṇa*), yet they are also read as an indigenous Deccan people **Sanskritised into Brahminhood** — the same "manufactured status" theme the Telugu notes trace forward.

### 8A.2 The political arc — the rulers who matter

- **Simuka → Kṛṣṇa → Sātakarṇi I**, the first great expander. The **Nāneghāṭ inscription of his queen Nāganikā** records **Vedic sacrifices (aśvamedha, rājasūya)** with huge priestly fees — **legitimation by śrauta ritual**.
- **Hāla** (c. 1st c. CE) — the **poet-king**, compiler of the **Gāthāsaptaśatī (Sattasaī)**, 700 Prākrit love-lyrics: a landmark of secular vernacular literature.
- **★ Gautamīputra Sātakarṇi (c. 106–130 CE)** — the **greatest**. The **Nāsik praśasti of his mother Gautamī Balaśrī** hails him as **destroyer of the Śakas, Yavanas and Pahlavas**, restorer of Brahmin prestige and the varṇa order, "**eka-brāhmaṇa**." He **crushed the Western Kṣatrapa (Śaka) Nahapāna** — **over-struck Nahapāna coins are the physical proof** — reclaiming the western Deccan.
- **Vāsiṣṭhīputra Puḷumāvi** — pushed power into the **eastern (Andhra) country**; **Amarāvatī / Dhānyakaṭaka** rises as a second centre; his coins reach the Coromandel.
- **Yajña Sātakarṇi** — the last strong king (**ships on his coins** = maritime power). After him the empire **fragments (3rd c. CE)** into successor houses — **Ikṣvākus, Ābhīras, Cutus, early Pallavas** — the **Ikṣvākus of Vijayapurī / Nāgārjunakoṇḍa** being the direct Andhra heirs and the bridge to the companion notes.

### 8A.3 The Śaka–Sātavāhana duel (a set-piece)

The century-long contest with the **Western Kṣatrapas (Śakas of Ujjain)** over the western Deccan and its ports is the spine of Sātavāhana politics: **Nahapāna's gains → Gautamīputra's reconquest → Rudradāman's counter-recovery** (his Junāgaḍh inscription, §8.3, boasts of twice defeating a "lord of Dakṣiṇāpatha" but sparing him as a kinsman-by-marriage) → renewed flux. **War and matrimony ran together** — a Sātavāhana married Rudradāman's daughter. It is the clearest Deccan instance of the §8 foreign relay meeting organised indigenous resistance.

### 8A.4 Administration, economy & coinage

- **Administration:** monarchy on a loosened Mauryan template — provinces (**āhāras**) under **amātyas / mahāmātras**, and a graded feudatory nobility (**rājā, mahārathi, mahābhoja, mahāsenāpati**). Crucially, the Sātavāhanas make the **first royal land-grants to Brahmins and Buddhist monks with fiscal-and-administrative immunity** (Nāsik) — the **seed of the later "feudal" grant economy** (R.S. Sharma) on which the Telugu-country notes build.
- **Economy & Roman trade:** the Deccan became the hinge of **Indo-Roman commerce** (the *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea* names the western ports **Kalyāṇa, Sopārā, Bharukaccha/Broach**); **Roman gold flowed in** (echoing §9's Kushāṇa trade). **Guilds (śreṇis) doubled as bankers** — Nāsik records **permanent endowments deposited with weaver- and oil-presser guilds at fixed interest**: precocious evidence of a monetised, institutional economy.
- **Coinage:** mostly **lead** and **potin** (also silver, copper); the **"ship-type" coins** advertise **maritime power**, and some carry **bilingual Prākrit–Dravidian legends** and **portrait heads** in imitation of their Kṣatrapa rivals.

### 8A.5 Society, religion & language

- **Society — the matronymic puzzle:** kings bear **metronymics** (*Gautamī-putra*, *Vāsiṣṭhī-putra*), naming after the mother's gotra. Read carefully: it signals the **importance of the mother's line, not matriarchy**; queens (Nāganikā, Gautamī Balaśrī) were prominent donors. Balaśrī's praśasti also frets about **varṇa-saṃkara (caste-mixing)** — evidence the four-fold order was being **loudly asserted precisely because the foreign influx had strained it**.
- **Religion — the double patronage:** the textbook case of **Brahmanical revival *and* Buddhist patronage together** — they **performed Vedic sacrifices** (Nāneghāṭ) and claimed to defend varṇāśrama, *yet* were **the greatest patrons of Buddhist stūpa-and-caitya building**. To them no contradiction: **royal legitimation was plural.**
- **Language — Prākrit's high-water mark:** official language **Prākrit in Brāhmī** — a pointed contrast with the **Sanskrit turn of their Śaka rivals** (Rudradāman's Junāgaḍh, §8.3). Royal patronage produced **Hāla's Gāthāsaptaśatī** and, by tradition, the **Kātantra** grammar (Sarvavarman).

### 8A.6 Art & architecture — the Amarāvatī school and the western caves

- **★ The Amarāvatī school** (Krishna valley — **Amarāvatī, Ghaṇṭaśāla, Nāgārjunakoṇḍa**, maturing under the Sātavāhanas and their Ikṣvāku successors): white-limestone stūpa-casing carved with **crowded, slender, sinuous, narrative reliefs** of the Buddha's life and the Jātakas — the **"third school" beside Gandhāra and Mathura** (§9.3), and the fountainhead of a South-Indian / Sri-Lankan / South-East-Asian style. **The Amarāvatī Mahāstūpa is Andhra's contribution to world Buddhist art.**
- **Western-Deccan rock-cut caityas & vihāras:** **Kārle** (the grandest caitya-hall), **Bhājā, Bedsā, Nāsik (Pāṇḍavleṇī), Kaṇheri, Junnar** — excavated on **guild and royal donation** and inscribed by Sātavāhana and Kṣatrapa donors. This is where the Barābar rock-cut idea (§7A.1) matures into monumental Buddhist architecture.

### 8A.7 Answer hooks

- **"The first Āndhra empire"** — Simuka to Yajña Sātakarṇi; **Gautamīputra Sātakarṇi** the greatest, **destroyer of Śaka-Yavana-Pahlava**, known through his **mother Gautamī Balaśrī's Nāsik praśasti**.
- **Bridge, not sideshow:** they **filled the Deccan vacuum after the Mauryas**, fought the **Śakas** (§8) to a standstill, traded with **Rome** like the Kushāṇas (§9), and **seeded the Ikṣvākus → the Andhra story** of the companion notes.
- **The signature paradoxes:** **Brahmanical sacrifice + Buddhist stūpa patronage**; **metronymics without matriarchy**; **Prākrit court against Śaka Sanskrit**.
- **Amarāvatī = the third sculptural school**; **Kārle / Nāsik = the western caityas** — Andhra and Maharashtra as twin art hubs.

## 9. The Kushāṇas (c. 1st – 3rd c. CE)

**Significance:** the Kushāṇas built the largest of the post-Mauryan empires — a **trans-continental state straddling Central Asia and North India** — and turned the subcontinent's north-west into the **hub of the Silk-Road world**, presiding over an age of **cultural synthesis, Mahāyāna Buddhism, and cosmopolitan art**.

### 9.1 Origins & rise

- The Kushāṇas were one of the **five clans of the Yuezhi (Ta-Yueh-chi)**, a Central-Asian people driven west by the Xiongnu. Under **Kujula Kadphises** they unified the clans and took Bactria/Kabul; **Vima Kadphises** pushed into north-west India and issued **abundant gold coinage** (reflecting **Roman-trade gold** and control of trans-Asian routes), styling himself a **devotee of Śiva (Maheśvara)** — a nice instance of a foreign dynasty adopting Indian religion.

### 9.2 Kaniṣka I — the great king (accession often dated c. 78 CE)

- **The empire at its height** stretched from **Central Asia (the Tarim basin) across Afghanistan and Gandhāra to the Ganga plain** — capitals at **Puruṣapura (Peshawar)** and **Mathura**. Kaniṣka is the classic "**bridge between East and West**."
- **The Śaka era (78 CE):** his accession is conventionally taken as the epoch of the **Śaka era**, adopted as **India's national calendar** — a standard exam fact (state it with the "conventionally/often attributed" caveat, as the dating is debated).
- **Patron of Buddhism (the second Ashoka):** he convened the **Fourth Buddhist Council** (Kashmir), under which **Mahāyāna Buddhism** crystallised and was **carried along the Silk Road into Central Asia and China** — the Kushāṇa empire being the conduit. His court hosted luminaries — **Aśvaghoṣa** (poet, *Buddhacarita*), **Nāgārjuna** (Mādhyamika philosophy, *śūnyatā*), **Vasumitra**, **Caraka** (medicine, the *Caraka-Saṃhitā*). *Note the parallel with Ashoka: a foreign king patronising Buddhism yet minting coins of Greek, Persian, and Indian deities alike — religious eclecticism as imperial glue.*

### 9.3 Kushāṇa cultural achievements — the analytical payload

- **Two schools of art (a guaranteed exam contrast):**

| | **Gandhāra school** | **Mathura school** |
|---|---|---|
| Region | North-west (Gandhāra) | Mathura (UP) |
| Influence | **Greco-Roman (Hellenistic)** form + Buddhist theme — "Greco-Buddhist" | **Indigenous** Indian idiom |
| Material | Grey schist (later stucco) | **Red sandstone** |
| Buddha image | Wavy hair, realistic muscular/anatomical drapery, serene classical features | Robust, energetic, shaven/spiral head, thin clinging robe, spiritual vigour |
| Note | Among the **first anthropomorphic Buddha images** | Independently produced the **first Buddha images** too; also carved Jain & Brahmanical icons |

  **Both schools flourished under Kushāṇa patronage and, for the first time, depicted the Buddha in human form** (earlier Buddhism used symbols — the wheel, footprints, bodhi-tree). This is a defining Kushāṇa contribution.

- **Trade & the Silk Road:** the Kushāṇa empire **controlled the overland Silk Road** linking **China, Central Asia, India and the Roman world**; **flourishing Indo-Roman trade** (Roman gold flowed in — Pliny complained of the drain; Indian ports and the *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea* attest it). Kushāṇa **gold coinage** (the finest before the Guptas, whom it influenced) is the material proof of this prosperity.
- **Religious eclecticism on the coins:** Kushāṇa coins depict **Greek (Helios, Herakles), Persian/Zoroastrian (Mithra, Mao, Ardoxsho), Indian (Śiva/Oesho, Buddha), and other deities** — a **cosmopolitan pantheon** that is itself a primary source for the age's syncretism.
- **The Saka–Kushana era's wider culture:** rise of **Mahāyāna and the Bodhisattva ideal**; the **Charaka Saṃhitā (medicine)**; Sanskrit gaining literary ground; a genuinely **international north India**.

### 9.4 Decline & legacy

- After Kaniṣka, successors (**Huviṣka, Vāsudeva** — note the **Indianised name**, marking assimilation) held on, but the empire **fragmented by the 3rd c. CE**, pressed by the rising **Sāsānians (Persia)** in the west and Indian powers in the east, dissolving before the **Guptas**.
- **Legacy:** (i) **Mahāyāna Buddhism exported to Central Asia & China** — arguably the Kushāṇas' greatest world-historical bequest; (ii) **Gandhāra & Mathura art**, incl. the first Buddha images; (iii) a template of **cosmopolitan, Silk-Road kingship**; (iv) **gold coinage** that shaped Gupta numismatics; (v) the **Śaka era**.

### 9.5 Answer hooks

- Kushāṇas = **India as the hub of the Silk-Road world**; a **trans-continental cultural bridge**.
- **Kaniṣka ≈ "the second Ashoka"**: Fourth Council, Mahāyāna, its export to China; scholars Aśvaghoṣa/Nāgārjuna/Caraka; **Śaka era (78 CE, conventionally)**.
- **Gandhāra (Greco-Roman, grey schist) vs. Mathura (indigenous, red sandstone)** — memorise the table; both give the **first human Buddha images**.
- **Coins as syncretism**: a multi-cultural pantheon minting religious eclecticism as imperial policy.

## 9A. Beyond the Kushāṇas — the Chinese Pilgrim-Travellers on Early India (Fa-Hien · Hiuen-Tsang · I-Tsing)

> **Why this section exists (a chronology note).** These travellers belong to the **Gupta–Harṣa age (5th–7th c. CE)**, *past* this document's Kushāṇa endpoint. It is included because the **2023 Paper-II question** — *"Examine the socio-religious life and administration of early India with respect to the writings of Chinese travellers such as Fa-Hien, Hiuen-Tsang and I-Tsang"* — demands it, and because it is the sharpest test of the **"foreign accounts as a source"** skill this whole document stresses (§0). All three were **Buddhist pilgrims, not neutral reporters** — that bias is itself the exam's real target.

### 9A.1 The three pilgrims at a glance

| Pilgrim *(Chinese)* | In India | Contemporary ruler | Work | Purpose / route |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fa-Hien** *(Faxian)* | c. 399–414 CE | **Chandragupta II "Vikramāditya"** (Gupta) | *Fo-kwo-ki* — *Record of Buddhist Kingdoms* | Collect Buddhist texts/Vinaya; **overland** via the Khyber, returned by sea |
| **Hiuen-Tsang** *(Xuanzang / Yuan Chwang)* | c. 630–645 CE | **Harṣavardhana** of Kanauj | *Si-yu-ki* — *Records of the Western World* | Study at **Nālandā** under **Śīlabhadra**; texts. The "**Prince of Pilgrims**" |
| **I-Tsing** *(Yijing; the paper's "I-Tsang")* | c. 671–695 CE | *(post-Harṣa)* | *A Record of the Buddhist Religion as Practised in India…* | Monastic discipline; ~10 yrs at **Nālandā**; came by **sea via Śrīvijaya** (Sumatra) |

### 9A.2 What they report — **administration**

- **Fa-Hien** on the **Gupta** state: a **mild, benevolent government** — rule **without corporal punishment** for most offences (**fines** the norm; repeat rebels lose a hand); **light land revenue (~1/6 of produce)** as the mainstay, so subjects were **un-registered, untaxed on movement, and free to come and go**; officials paid from crown lands; roads generally **safe**; and **charitable hospitals and rest-houses** at Pāṭaliputra endowed by rich citizens — real **public welfare**. He marvels at the **peace and prosperity of Madhyadeśa**. *(Tellingly, he never names the king — a Buddhist pilgrim indifferent to Hindu Gupta politics: flag this bias.)*
- **Hiuen-Tsang** on **Harṣa**: a **tireless, benevolent monarch** perpetually touring his realm; revenue **light (~1/6)**, state income split four ways (administration, officials' salaries, scholars, charity); the great **quinquennial assembly (mahāmokṣa-pariṣad) at Prayāga**, where Harṣa gave away his treasury, and the **Kanauj religious assembly**; **but roads far less safe** than in Fa-Hien's day — Hiuen-Tsang was **himself robbed**, a telling sign of post-Gupta disorder.
- **I-Tsing:** less on the crown, more on the **administration of the monastery-universities** — how endowed villages funded **Nālandā** and its **~3,000+ resident monks**, the **strict entrance debates**, daily routine and discipline, and the sea-network binding India to **Śrīvijaya** as a co-equal Buddhist study-hub.

### 9A.3 What they report — **socio-religious life**

- **Varṇa and untouchability:** **Fa-Hien** records the **Chaṇḍālas** dwelling **outside the towns** and **striking a piece of wood** on entering so others could avoid their "polluting" touch — the **earliest foreign testimony to untouchability**. **Hiuen-Tsang** confirms a **rigid four-varṇa order**, with butchers, executioners and scavengers living apart.
- **Religion — coexistence, and Buddhism's regional decline:** both note **Buddhism and Brahmanism flourishing side by side**, yet **Hiuen-Tsang sees Buddhism decaying in many regions** (ruined monasteries) even as **Nālandā blazed** — the hinge toward Buddhism's eventual disappearance in India (§5.3). **Harṣa's tolerance** (patron of Śiva, Sūrya *and* the Buddha) is attested.
- **Society and learning:** upper-order **vegetarianism** (Fa-Hien: onion, garlic and liquor shunned; only Chaṇḍālas hunt and eat meat); thriving towns and guilds; and **Nālandā** as an **international university** teaching logic, grammar, medicine and metaphysics.
- **Source-criticism — the real payload:** as **devout Buddhists** they **over-report Buddhist prosperity, credit miracles, and treat Hindu kingship with indifference.** **Hiuen-Tsang** is the careful geographer (distances, directions, crops), **Fa-Hien** the impressionistic pilgrim, **I-Tsing** the narrowly monastic observer. **Use them as corroboration, cross-checked against inscriptions and coins — never as neutral fact.** That is exactly the methodological rule of §0.

### 9A.4 Answer hooks

- **"Foreign eyes, Buddhist lens"** — invaluable but biased: over-bright on Buddhism, indifferent to Hindu polity.
- **Administration:** all three attest **mild rule, ~1/6 land revenue, fines over mutilation, public charity** — but the **decline in road-safety from Fa-Hien to Hiuen-Tsang** neatly tracks the **Gupta → post-Gupta slide**.
- **Society:** the **Chaṇḍāla passages are the earliest external evidence of untouchability**; rigid varṇa; Buddhism–Brahmanism coexistence with **Buddhism regionally declining, Nālandā ascendant.**
- **Anchor each to a reign:** **Fa-Hien → Chandragupta II; Hiuen-Tsang → Harṣa; I-Tsing → Nālandā/Śrīvijaya.**

## 10. Synthesis: the through-lines to carry into the exam hall

An examiner rewards a candidate who can **connect the sections**. Four master-threads run through the whole arc:

1. **Urbanisation comes in two waves, both surplus-driven.** Harappa (first) and the sixth-century-BCE Ganga cities (second) both rest on an **agrarian surplus + trade + a regulating authority**. In between lies the **de-urbanised Vedic pastoral-agrarian interlude**. *Cities are a function of surplus and organisation, not of "progress."*

2. **Material change drives social and religious change.** **Iron + plough → surplus → states → stratification (varṇa) → the discontent that produced Jainism & Buddhism → the empires that patronised them.** Never present religion or politics as free-floating; anchor them to the economic base.

3. **Legitimation evolves.** Vedic **sacrifice** → Mauryan **bureaucracy + Ashokan Dhamma (ethical kingship)** → Kushāṇa **coin-iconography and religious eclecticism** — with the **Sātavāhanas' simultaneous Vedic sacrifice and Buddhist stūpa-patronage** (§8A.5) as the Deccan variant. Each age invents a new **software of rule** to bind a widening polity.

4. **The evidence dictates the argument.** Undeciphered script (Harappa) = no politics; priestly texts (Vedic) = elite-biased; contemporary edicts (Ashoka) = a ruler's own voice; coins (Indo-Greeks, Kushāṇas) = chronology and syncretism. **Lead every answer by naming your source and its limits** — it is the surest mark of a Mains-level historian.

### 11.3 Pattern takeaways (what the PYQs tell you to prioritise)

1. **The Harappan unit is the single densest source of questions** — town planning + causes of decline + Indus-vs-Vedic comparison are near-perennial. Prepare §2 to full-answer depth.
2. **Jainism/Buddhism is tested as causation and comparison**, not narration — "causes of emergence," "factors for rise and growth," "Hīnayāna vs. Mahāyāna + decline." §5's causal framing and comparison tables map directly onto this.
3. **The examiner's verbs are analytical** — *critically examine, compare and contrast, analyse the factors, evaluate.* Bare description scores poorly; use the "answer hooks" and debates in each section.

## 12. Historiography — how we know, and who argued what

*An answer that names its historian and its school reads as graduate-level. This is the toolkit — and it gathers the debates scattered through the document into one place.*

### 12.1 The sources, one more time (the typology to lead with)

| Source type | Gives you | Systematic limit |
|---|---|---|
| **Archaeology** (tools, cities, pottery, aDNA) | material culture, chronology, economy | rarely names, gods, motives, politics |
| **Inscriptions** (edicts, praśastis, grants) | official self-image, firm dates, land | eulogy, not neutral fact; donor-biased |
| **Coins** (numismatics) | chronology, kings, economy, religion, art | attribution & dating hard for early issues |
| **Texts** (Veda, Buddhist/Jain canon, Arthaśāstra, Purāṇa, Sangam) | ideals, doctrine, prescription | later redaction; elite/priestly bias |
| **Foreign accounts** (Megasthenes, *Periplus*, Chinese pilgrims) | an external check; vivid description | outsider error and bias (§9A) |

### 12.2 The interpretive schools (name them, weigh them)

- **Orientalist / Colonial** — Jones, **Prinsep** (who cracked Brāhmī, §7), **Marshall** (Harappa), **Vincent Smith**: recovered and dated the past, but framed India as **static, despotic and other-worldly**, needing an outside stimulus (the "civilisation came from the west" reflex — now rejected, §2.6).
- **Nationalist** — **R.G. Bhandarkar, H.C. Raychaudhuri, R.C. Majumdar, K.A. Nilakanta Sastri, K.P. Jayaswal** (whose *Hindu Polity* rehabilitated the **republics**, §4.2a): answered colonial denigration by recovering indigenous achievement; risk = **glorification**.
- **Marxist / materialist** — **D.D. Kosambi, R.S. Sharma, Romila Thapar, Irfan Habib**: read history through **modes of production, class and material change**. This document's "**iron → surplus → state → stratification → heterodoxy**" spine (§3, §10) is theirs; so is Sharma's "**feudal**" reading of land-grants (§8A.4) and his urban-decay thesis.
- **Subaltern & social history** — recover the excluded (women, śūdras, tribes, the everyday); Thapar's *lineage-to-state*, and (in the companion Telugu notes) **Cynthia Talbot** on caste and gender.
- **Hindutva / indigenist** — the "**OIT / Sarasvatī**" school (indigenous Aryans, Harappan-as-Vedic): **politically charged**; handle with the §3.4 balance.

### 12.3 The set-piece debates to be able to cite (an index)

- **Aryan origins** — migration vs indigenous; linguistics + archaeology + aDNA (§3.4).
- **Harappan polity & script** — a state, or stateless? undeciphered, hence "faceless" (§2.3, §2.3a).
- **Harappan decline** — multi-causal; the Aryan-invasion/"massacre" thesis discredited (§2.4).
- **Mauryan art** — Persepolitan borrowing vs indigenous idiom (§7A.2).
- **Mauryan decline** — Ashokan pacifism (Haraprasad Sastri) vs structural over-centralisation (§6.3, §7.4).
- **Sātavāhana** origins, homeland and varṇa (§8A.1).
- **The "78 CE" era-problem** — the Śaka era and Kaniṣka's date (§8.3, §9.2).
- **Foreign accounts as evidence** — Megasthenes' "seven castes"; the Chinese pilgrims' Buddhist lens (§6.4, §9A).
