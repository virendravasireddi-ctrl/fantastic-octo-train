## 0. Why this topic matters

"Dimensions of ethics" is asked two ways: (a) the **four branches** of ethical study (descriptive, normative, meta-ethics, applied) — the textbook answer; and (b) the **normative theories** inside the second branch (duty vs consequence vs character), which power every GS-IV case-study justification. Learn both, plus the **Indian ethical traditions** — examiners reward answers that pair Kant/Mill with Gita/Kautilya.

---

## 1. The four dimensions (branches) of ethics ⭐

| Dimension | Question it asks | Nature | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Descriptive ethics** | What *do* people actually believe and practise? | Empirical/scientific — describes, does not judge | Anthropology of marriage customs; surveys on attitudes to bribery; Kohlberg's stages |
| **2. Normative (prescriptive) ethics** | What *ought* people to do? By what standard? | Prescriptive — builds theories of right action | Deontology, consequentialism, virtue ethics, justice theories (§2) |
| **3. Meta-ethics** | What do "good," "right," "ought" even *mean*? Are moral truths objective? | Analytical/second-order — the philosophy *about* ethics | Moral realism vs relativism; G. E. Moore's "**naturalistic fallacy**" (*Principia Ethica*, 1903); emotivism (Ayer); Hume's **is–ought gap** |
| **4. Applied ethics** | How do norms apply to *specific fields and dilemmas*? | Practical, interdisciplinary | Bioethics (euthanasia, surrogacy), environmental ethics, business ethics, media ethics, ethics of war, **AI/data ethics**, administrative ethics — the civil servant's field |

Memory hook: **D-N-M-A** — *Describe what is, Norm what ought, Mean the meanings, Apply to fields.*

## 2. Inside normative ethics — the three great families ⭐⭐

### 2.1 Deontology — duty ethics (the act itself)

- **Immanuel Kant** (*Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*, 1785): morality = acting from **duty**, discoverable by pure reason; consequences are irrelevant to rightness.
- **Categorical imperative** — formulations to quote: (i) **universalisability** — act only on a maxim you can will as universal law (could *everyone* lie?); (ii) **humanity as end** — treat persons never merely as means; (iii) autonomy — the rational will legislates for itself.
- Strengths: dignity of the individual, absolute floors (no torture, no false witness), rule-of-law temperament — the natural ethic of a **civil servant bound by the Constitution**.
- Critiques: rigidity (Kant's "no lie even to a murderer at the door"), conflicts of duties unresolved, blind to outcomes.
- Indian parallel: ***nishkama karma*** of the **Bhagavad Gita** (2.47 — *karmanye vadhikaraste ma phaleshu*): act by duty (*svadharma*), renounce attachment to fruits — duty-ethics with a spiritual engine.

### 2.2 Teleology / consequentialism — outcome ethics (the results)

- Rightness = whatever produces the **best consequences**.
- **Jeremy Bentham** (*Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, 1789): **utilitarianism** — "the **greatest happiness of the greatest number**"; pleasure/pain the only measures; the **felicific calculus** (intensity, duration, certainty…); quantitative — "pushpin is as good as poetry."
- **J. S. Mill** (*Utilitarianism*, 1863): **qualitative** hedonism — higher (intellectual) pleasures outrank lower; "better to be **a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied**; better Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied."
- Act vs rule utilitarianism: judge each act by utility vs follow the rules that maximise utility overall (rule-version answers the "punish an innocent to calm a mob?" objection).
- Strengths: welfare-focused, natural language of **public policy** (cost–benefit analysis, triage, vaccine prioritisation).
- Critiques: can trample minority rights ("tyranny of the majority"), measurement problems, ends-justify-means slide — **Gandhi's rebuttal:** impure means poison the end.
- Indian parallels: Kautilya's welfare consequentialism (*praja-sukhe sukham rajnah*); Buddhist compassion aimed at reducing suffering (*dukkha*).

### 2.3 Virtue ethics — character (the agent)

- **Aristotle** (*Nicomachean Ethics*): ask not "what should I do?" but "**what kind of person should I be?**" Virtue (*arete*) is a **habituated disposition**; each virtue a **golden mean** between excesses — courage between cowardice and rashness, liberality between miserliness and prodigality; the goal is *eudaimonia* (flourishing); **practical wisdom (*phronesis*)** finds the mean in each situation.
- Strengths: explains moral education and role models; fits civil-service "foundational values" (integrity, empathy are *virtues*, not rules).
- Critiques: vague action-guidance; virtues culturally variable.
- Indian parallels: **dharma** as cultivated righteousness; the Gita's *sthitaprajna* (person of steady wisdom); Buddhism's **Middle Path** (a golden mean) and *panchasheela*; Jainism's *ahimsa, aparigraha, anekantavada* (non-violence, non-possession, many-sidedness of truth).

### 2.4 Justice- and rights-based additions (name-drop tier)

- **John Rawls** (*A Theory of Justice*, 1971): **justice as fairness** — principles chosen behind a **veil of ignorance**; equal basic liberties + the **difference principle** (inequalities only if they benefit the least advantaged) — the philosophical spine of affirmative action.
- **Robert Nozick** — entitlement/rights libertarianism (counterpoint).
- **Care ethics** — **Carol Gilligan** (*In a Different Voice*, 1982), Nel Noddings: morality of relationships, empathy and responsibility — corrective to abstract rule-ethics; feeds "compassion towards weaker sections."
- **Amartya Sen** (*The Idea of Justice*, 2009): compare *actual* social realisations, not ideal institutions — *nyaya* over *niti*.

### 2.5 The three families at a glance (case-study toolkit)

| | Deontology | Consequentialism | Virtue ethics |
|---|---|---|---|
| Focus | The **act** and the rule | The **outcome** | The **agent's character** |
| Question | Is it my duty? Universalisable? | Does it maximise welfare? | What would a person of integrity do? |
| Champion | Kant; Gita's *nishkama karma* | Bentham, Mill; Kautilya | Aristotle; dharma traditions |
| Civil-service echo | Rule of law, conduct rules | Cost–benefit, public interest | Foundational values, role models |
| Weakness | Rigid | Rights can be trampled | Vague guidance |

**GS-IV craft:** a full-credit case-study justification runs all three tests on your chosen option — duty/legality, consequences for all stakeholders, and what it does to your character/precedent.

## 3. Other useful "dimensions" framings

Examiners sometimes intend *levels* or *spheres* rather than branches; keep this alternate scheme ready:

1. **Individual dimension** — personal integrity, conscience, self-regarding virtues.
2. **Interpersonal/private dimension** — family, friendship, neighbourly duties (File 03).
3. **Professional/organisational dimension** — codes of conduct, workplace ethics, administrative ethics.
4. **Social/public dimension** — citizenship, constitutional morality, tolerance, social justice.
5. **Global/intergenerational dimension** — climate duty to future generations, humanitarian law, refugee ethics; **environmental ethics** (Stockholm 1972 → Paris 2015; Art. 48A & 51A(g)).
6. **Ethics and technology** — AI bias, surveillance, data protection (DPDP Act 2023) — the newest interface.

## 4. Indian ethical thought — the ready-reckoner ⭐

| Source | Core ethical idea | Quotable |
|---|---|---|
| **Rigveda / Upanishads** | ***Rta*** (cosmic order) → *satya*; "**Satyameva Jayate**" (Mundaka Upanishad — national motto) | Truth as the highest law |
| **Purusharthas** | Four life-goals — **dharma, artha, kama, moksha** — wealth and desire *bounded by* dharma | Ethics as the regulator of acquisition |
| **Bhagavad Gita** | ***Nishkama karma***; *svadharma*; equanimity (*samatva*) | "You have a right to action alone, never to its fruits" |
| **Buddhism** | Four Noble Truths; **Eightfold Path** (right speech, action, livelihood…); *panchasheela*; **Middle Path**; *karuna* (compassion) | Ethics as the cure for suffering |
| **Jainism** | ***Ahimsa*** as supreme dharma; *aparigraha*; ***anekantavada*** (intellectual humility) | Non-violence in thought, word, deed |
| **Kautilya (Arthashastra)** | Statecraft with welfare telos; king's vows; anti-corruption realism (40 ways of embezzlement) | *Praja-sukhe sukham rajnah* |
| **Ashoka** | ***Dhamma*** — toleration (RE XII), welfare, *dhamma-mahamattas* (the first "ethics officers") | Ethics institutionalised in administration |
| **Tirukkural (Thiruvalluvar)** | Virtue (*aram*), wealth, love — universal secular ethics | Quoted in Parliament & GS-IV alike |
| **Gandhi** | Truth (*satya*) + non-violence (*ahimsa*); means–end unity; **trusteeship** of wealth; *sarvodaya/antyodaya* | The Talisman |
| **Vivekananda** | *Daridra-narayana* — service of the poor is worship; strength and fearlessness as virtues | "They alone live who live for others" |
| **Constitution as ethical document** | Preamble values; FRs; DPSPs; **Fundamental Duties (51A)**; **constitutional morality** (Ambedkar: must be *cultivated*, "not a natural sentiment") | The modern Indian dharma-text |
