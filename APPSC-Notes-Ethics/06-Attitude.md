## 0. Why this topic matters

Attitude is the syllabus's social-psychology core (APPSC item 13; GS-IV verbatim). It supplies the machinery questions: **content/structure** (ABC), **functions** (Katz — the single most repeated list), the **attitude–behaviour gap** (LaPiere, dissonance, planned behaviour), **moral vs political attitudes**, and **social influence & persuasion** (Asch, Milgram, Cialdini, ELM) — plus the governance payoff: how the state changes citizen attitudes (Swachh Bharat, nudges) and how officers keep the *right* attitudes (non-partisanship, empathy).

## 1. Meaning and content (structure)

- **Gordon Allport (1935)** ⭐ — the exam definition: an attitude is "a **mental and neural state of readiness**, organised through experience, exerting a **directive or dynamic influence** upon the individual's response to all objects and situations with which it is related." (Allport also called attitude "the most distinctive and indispensable concept" in social psychology.)
- Working shorthand: **a learned evaluative predisposition** — a stored like/dislike toward an "attitude object" (person, group, policy, idea).
- **Attitude vs value vs belief vs opinion:** belief = cognition held true (no evaluation); value = deep trans-situational standard (few, central); attitude = evaluation of a *specific* object (many, derived from values); opinion = verbal expression of an attitude. Values → attitudes → opinions/behaviour is the standard cascade.

### 1.1 The ABC (tripartite) model of content ⭐⭐

| Component | What it holds | Example: attitude toward corruption |
|---|---|---|
| **A — Affective** | Feelings/emotions toward the object | Disgust at bribe-taking |
| **B — Behavioural (conative)** | Action tendencies/past behaviour | Refusing to pay; reporting |
| **C — Cognitive** | Beliefs, thoughts, attributes | "Corruption steals from the poor" |

- Components can conflict (**ambivalence**): a smoker *knows* (C) smoking kills yet *enjoys* it (A) — predicting which wins is the attitude–behaviour problem of §3.
- Attitudes vary in **valence** (for/against), **extremity**, **strength/centrality** (strong attitudes resist change, persist, predict behaviour), **accessibility** (speed of activation), and may be **explicit** (self-reported) or **implicit** (automatic; measured by the Implicit Association Test — Greenwald) — implicit bias is why fair-minded officers can still discriminate, and why procedures (blind evaluation) matter.

### 1.2 Formation of attitudes

**Direct experience** (strongest, most accessible) · **classical conditioning** (object paired with feeling — advertising) · **operant conditioning** (praise/punishment for expressed views) · **observational learning/modelling** (Bandura — family first, then peers, media) · **mere exposure** (Zajonc, 1968 — familiarity alone breeds liking) · **group norms and socialisation** (schools, religion, workplace culture).

## 2. Functions of attitude — Daniel Katz (1960) ⭐⭐ (the must-know list)

| Function | The attitude serves to… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Knowledge** | Organise and simplify a complex world; frame of reference | Stereotypes as (dangerous) cognitive shortcuts |
| **2. Adjustment / utilitarian / instrumental** | Gain rewards, avoid punishments | Pro-boss attitudes in a hierarchical office |
| **3. Ego-defensive** | Protect self-esteem from uncomfortable truths | Blaming victims to preserve "just world" belief; projection |
| **4. Value-expressive** | Express core values and identity | Wearing khadi; vegetarianism; publicly refusing dowry |

**Application twist for Mains:** attitude change must target the *function* — a utilitarian attitude changes with incentives; an ego-defensive one needs threat-reduction, not facts; a value-expressive one changes only if reframed as *consistent* with the person's values (e.g., pitching toilet-building as *izzat*/dignity in Swachh Bharat, not as sanitation engineering).

## 3. Attitude, thought and behaviour — the influence question

### 3.1 Attitudes shape thought

Attitudes act as filters: **selective attention, perception and recall** (we notice and remember attitude-consistent information), **confirmation bias**, assimilation of ambiguous facts to prior evaluation — why polarised publics read the same news differently, and why officers must institutionalise objectivity (recorded reasons, data) rather than trust neutrality of perception.

### 3.2 Do attitudes predict behaviour? The gap

- **LaPiere (1934)** ⭐ — travelled the US with a Chinese couple; served at 250+ establishments; yet 90%+ of the same establishments *wrote* they would refuse Chinese guests — the classic demonstration that stated attitudes ≠ behaviour.
- **When attitudes DO predict behaviour** (moderators): attitude and behaviour measured at the **same specificity** (Ajzen–Fishbein compatibility principle); **strong, accessible, direct-experience** attitudes; low situational pressure; low self-monitoring individuals; vested interest.
- **Theory of Planned Behaviour (Ajzen)** ⭐: **intention** drives behaviour, and intention = **attitude toward the act + subjective norms (what others expect) + perceived behavioural control**. Governance use: to change behaviour (helmet-wearing, tax filing), work on all three — persuasion (attitude), social campaigns (norms), and *ease* (control: online filing, helmet availability).

### 3.3 Behaviour shapes attitudes (the reverse arrow)

- **Cognitive dissonance — Leon Festinger (1957)** ⭐⭐: inconsistency between cognitions (or act vs attitude) produces aversive arousal; we reduce it by changing the attitude, the behaviour, or adding justifications. **Festinger & Carlsmith (1959)**: paid **$1 vs $20** to call a boring task fun — the *$1* group came to believe it (insufficient external justification forces internal change).
  - Administrative uses: induce public commitments (ODF pledges) — behaviour drags attitude along; beware its dark side — officers who commit small irregularities rationalise larger ones ("slippery slope" of corruption is dissonance-reduction).
- **Self-perception theory (Daryl Bem)**: with weak prior attitudes we *infer* our attitudes from our own behaviour ("I volunteered, so I must care") — the logic of foot-in-the-door.

## 4. Moral attitudes

- **Definition:** attitudes whose object is judged in terms of **right/wrong** rather than mere like/dislike — anchored in conscience and values (honesty, justice, dignity of persons, non-violence).
- **Distinctive features:** experienced as **universal and obligatory** (not preferences); charged with **moral emotions** (guilt, indignation, elevation); **stronger, more central, more resistant** to persuasion; violations by others provoke punishment-willingness (costly enforcement).
- Formation: Kohlberg's ladder (File 01), family induction, moral exemplars; **moral disengagement** (Bandura) explains how good people do bad things — euphemism ("collateral damage"), diffusion of responsibility, dehumanisation — the checklist for how institutional corruption sustains itself.
- Civil-service relevance: moral attitudes are the raw material of **integrity**; but *rigid* moralism without prudence can become self-righteousness — the balanced officer pairs strong moral attitude with procedural fairness.

## 5. Political attitudes

- **Definition:** evaluative orientations toward political objects — parties, ideologies, leaders, institutions, policies (e.g., left–right economic attitudes, liberal–conservative social attitudes).
- **Sources (political socialisation):** family (strongest early predictor of party identification), education, region/caste/class/religion, media and social media (echo chambers, filter bubbles), generational events (Emergency, liberalisation, movements).
- Classic study: **Adorno et al., *The Authoritarian Personality* (1950) — the F-scale** ⭐: harsh parenting → deference to authority + aggression to outgroups; refined later as **right-wing authoritarianism (Altemeyer)**. Also: political apathy/cynicism as attitudes corroding democratic participation.
- **The civil servant's mandated political attitude:** **non-partisanship** (no political attitude *in action*), **neutrality** across governments, commitment to the **Constitution** rather than to any ideology — political attitudes are for the citizen-self at the ballot box, never for the office. (Conduct Rules bar political activity; the anonymity–neutrality frame of File 04.)

## 6. Social influence — how others change us ⭐

Three escalating forms: **conformity** (implicit group pressure) → **compliance** (explicit request) → **obedience** (command of authority).

| Phenomenon | Landmark study | Result / concept |
|---|---|---|
| **Conformity (norm formation)** | **Sherif (1935)** — autokinetic effect | Ambiguity → group norms emerge and persist |
| **Conformity (against evidence)** | **Asch (1951/55)** — line-length judgments | ~**75%** conformed at least once to an obviously wrong majority; ~⅓ of all responses conformed |
| Why we conform | **Deutsch & Gerard (1955)** | **Informational** influence (want to be right) vs **normative** influence (want to be liked) |
| **Obedience** | **Milgram (1961–63)** — "shock" experiments | **65%** administered the maximum 450V on command — ordinary people, destructive obedience |
| Situational power | **Zimbardo (1971)** — Stanford Prison | Roles and situations corrupt; guards vs prisoners |
| **Minority influence** | **Moscovici (1969)** | A *consistent, confident* minority converts majorities — the reformer's mechanism (Gandhi as case) |
| **Compliance techniques** | **Cialdini, *Influence* (1984)** ⭐ | Six principles: **reciprocity, commitment/consistency, social proof, liking, authority, scarcity** (+ later "unity") |

- Named tactics: **foot-in-the-door** (small request → large), **door-in-the-face** (large refused → moderate accepted), **low-balling** — recognise them as citizen and resist them as officer (vendor hospitality is a reciprocity trap — the conduct-rules logic).
- **Administrative morals of the research:** Milgram/Zimbardo explain how ordinary officials execute unethical orders — the counters are institutional: written dissent, illegal-order refusal, whistle-blower protection, diffuse authority. Asch explains groupthink in committees — the counter is a designated devil's advocate and secret ballots. Moscovici licenses the honest minority officer: consistency converts.

## 7. Persuasion — deliberate attitude change

- **Yale attitude-change approach (Hovland, 1950s):** persuasion = **who** (source: credibility = expertise + trustworthiness; attractiveness; the *sleeper effect* — low-credibility messages gain force as source is forgotten) × **what** (message: moderate discrepancy, two-sided for informed audiences, **fear appeals work only with efficacy instructions**) × **to whom** (audience: intelligence, self-esteem, forewarning).
- **Elaboration Likelihood Model — Petty & Cacioppo (1986)** ⭐⭐: two routes —
  - **Central route:** high motivation + ability → careful processing of *arguments* → durable, behaviour-predictive change.
  - **Peripheral route:** low elaboration → cues (celebrity, length, likes) → shallow, temporary change.
  - Governance rule of thumb: use central-route material (data, deliberation, jan sunwais) for durable change; peripheral cues (brand ambassadors, jingles) only to open the door.
- **Resistance:** **inoculation theory (McGuire)** — pre-exposure to weakened counter-arguments builds immunity (the design behind **prebunking** of fake news); **psychological reactance (Brehm)** — heavy-handed persuasion boomerangs ("forbidden fruit"), why bans often backfire and framing beats fiat.
- **Persuasion in Indian governance (examples bank):**
  - **Swachh Bharat** — triggering disgust + *izzat* framing + celebrity cues + social-norm signalling (ODF villages) — the full ELM/Katz toolkit in one mission.
  - **Nudge:** Economic Survey **2018-19** chapter on behavioural economics ("thinking of the citizen as *Homo sapiens*, not *Homo economicus*") — defaults (Give-It-Up LPG), social proof in tax letters ("9 of 10 in your area pay on time"), BBBP's *Selfie with Daughter*.
  - COVID-19 vaccination drives — fear-with-efficacy messaging, local influencers, convenience (TPB's "control" leg).
- **Ethics of persuasion:** persuasion respects autonomy (reasons offered); **manipulation** bypasses it (exploiting biases covertly); **propaganda** adds deception at scale. The public servant may persuade and nudge transparently, never manipulate — publicity test applies (File 03).
