## 1. The Śuṅgas (185–73 BCE) and the Kāṇvas (73–28 BCE)

**Significance:** the Śuṅgas are the **first post-Mauryan dynasty of the Gangetic heartland** and the classic case of a **Brahmanical revival** after the heterodox-friendly Mauryas — yet, read carefully, they are *not* the anti-Buddhist persecutors that later legend makes them. They also **held the north-west frontier against the Indo-Greeks** and presided over the **first great phase of Buddhist stone art**.

### 1.1 The core narrative

- **The coup (185 BCE).** **Puṣyamitra Śuṅga**, the Brahmin **senāpati (commander-in-chief)** of the last Maurya **Bṛhadratha**, assassinated him at an **army review** and seized the throne (see companion §6.3). Capital at **Pāṭaliputra**, with **Vidiśā** (eastern Malwa) as a key secondary seat — his son **Agnimitra** was viceroy there (the hero of Kālidāsa's play *Mālavikāgnimitram*).
- **The Indo-Greek challenge.** The **Bactrian/Indo-Greeks** (Demetrius, then **Menander**) pushed toward the Ganga plain; the *Yuga Purāṇa* (part of the *Gārgī Saṃhitā*) and the *Mālavikāgnimitram* record their advance to **Mathura / near Pāṭaliputra** and their repulse by Puṣyamitra's grandson **Vasumitra** on the Sindhu (a tributary). Kalinga's **Khāravela** (Hāthīgumphā inscription) also pressed Magadha in this period.
- **The Vedic revival.** Puṣyamitra performed **two aśvamedha (horse-sacrifices)** — attested by the **Ayodhyā inscription of Dhanadeva** and by the grammarian **Patañjali**, who says *"iha Puṣyamitraṃ yājayāmaḥ"* ("here we perform the sacrifice for Puṣyamitra"). The aśvamedha is the **signature of a self-conscious Brahmanical, Vedic-ritual kingship** reasserted after Mauryan/Ashokan patronage of the heterodoxies.
- **The Kāṇvas (73–28 BCE).** The last Śuṅga, **Devabhūti**, was murdered by his Brahmin minister **Vasudeva Kāṇva**, who founded the short **Kāṇva/Kāṇvāyana** line — four kings, ~45 years — until, per the Purāṇas, the **Sātavāhanas ("Andhras," §2) overthrew them** c. 28 BCE.

### 1.2 The evidence — and its bias

- **Patañjali's *Mahābhāṣya*** — a rare **contemporary** witness (grammar, but it dates and names the aśvamedha).
- **The Purāṇas** — dynastic lists (10 Śuṅga kings, ~112 years).
- **Kālidāsa's *Mālavikāgnimitram*** — a **later, literary, courtly** source (Gupta age) — use with care, but it preserves the Vasumitra/Yavana episode.
- **The *Yuga Purāṇa*** and the **Ayodhyā (Dhanadeva) inscription** — for the Indo-Greek war and the aśvamedha.
- **The monuments** — **Bhārhut** (railings, Jātaka reliefs) and the **Sāñchī** stūpa's enlargement and railings — the material record.
- **The Buddhist *Divyāvadāna* / *Aśokāvadāna*** — **hostile**: it charges Puṣyamitra with destroying stūpas and pricing monks' heads. *This is the source of the "persecutor" image — and it is sectarian.*

### 1.3 Analysis & debates

- **"Brahmanical revival," not "Buddhist persecution."** The persecution charge (Divyāvadāna) is almost certainly **sectarian exaggeration**, because **Buddhist art flourished under the Śuṅgas**: the **Bhārhut** reliefs and the **Sāñchī** railings are Śuṅga-age. The honest verdict: the Śuṅgas **withdrew royal patronage from Buddhism and re-centred Vedic ritual — a *revival*, not a *pogrom*.** (Loss of patronage ≠ persecution: a transferable exam line.)
- **A revivalist cultural moment.** The era is associated with the consolidation of **Sanskrit** and of Brahmanical texts — Patañjali's grammar; the *Gārgī Saṃhitā*; and (by many datings) the **Manusmṛti** — the codification of the varṇa order that the heterodox age had challenged.
- **Art-historical landmark.** **Bhārhut and Sāñchī** give us **early Indian narrative relief** (Jātaka storytelling in stone) and are still **aniconic** — the Buddha shown by **symbols** (wheel, footprints, empty throne, bodhi-tree), *before* the human Buddha image of the Kushāṇa-age Gandhāra/Mathura schools (companion §9.3). The Śuṅga phase is the **seedbed of Indian stone architecture**.

### 1.4 Answer hooks

- Śuṅgas = the **Brahmanical/Vedic revival** after the Mauryas — **aśvamedha** (Patañjali, Ayodhyā inscription), Sanskrit, Manusmṛti-milieu — but **not** wholesale Buddhist persecution: **Bhārhut & Sāñchī flourished** (Divyāvadāna's charge is sectarian).
- Frontier: **repelled the Indo-Greeks** (Vasumitra), per the *Mālavikāgnimitram* and *Yuga Purāṇa*.
- Art: **Bhārhut & Sāñchī** — early **aniconic** Buddhist narrative relief; the start of Indian stone art.
- **Kāṇvas** = a Brahmin-minister coda (Vasudeva killed Devabhūti), ended by the **Sātavāhanas**.

## 2. The Sātavāhanas (c. 1st c. BCE – 3rd c. CE)

**Significance:** the Sātavāhanas built the **first great empire of the Deccan** and became the **bridge between the Mauryan north and the Tamil south** — politically, commercially (Indo-Roman trade) and culturally (Prakrit, the Amaravati art school). For APPSC they are **doubly examinable**: here as an **all-India Deccan power** (Part A, Unit 1), and — as the founding dynasty of Andhra history — as a **Part-B (Andhra) staple** (see the Part-B PYQs in the syllabus map, and the companion Telugu-country notes). *Prepare them to full depth.*

### 2.1 The core narrative

- **Identity & the "Andhra" question.** The Purāṇas call them the **Andhras / Andhra-bhṛtyas**; their inscriptions call them **Sātavāhana**. Founder **Simuka** (who, per the Purāṇas, overthrew the Kāṇvas/Śuṅgas). Capital at **Pratiṣṭhāna (Paithan** on the Godāvarī**)**.
- **The homeland debate (a live exam point).** Did they originate in the **western Deccan** (Maharashtra — where the earliest inscriptions, Nāsik and Naneghat, cluster) or the **eastern Deccan** (the Krishna–Godāvarī Andhra country — the Purāṇic "Andhra" label)? The safe line: *western concentration of early records vs. the eastern "Andhra" name — the question is unsettled; they ended up ruling both.*
- **The Śaka crisis and the great restoration.** The dynasty was pushed back by the **Śaka Western Kṣatrapa Nahapāna**, then dramatically restored by **Gautamīputra Sātakarṇi** (c. early 2nd c. CE), the greatest king. His mother **Gautamī Balaśrī's Nāsik praśasti** eulogises him as the **destroyer of the Śakas, Yavanas and Pahlavas**, restorer of Sātavāhana glory. His defeat of **Nahapāna** is confirmed materially by the **Jogalthambi hoard** — Nahapāna's silver coins **restruck** by Gautamīputra.
- **Consolidation and peak.** His son **Vāsiṣṭhīputra Puḷumāvi** extended power **eastward into the Andhra (Krishna–Godāvarī)** country (coins and the Amaravati region). The last notable king, **Yajña Śrī Sātakarṇi**, issued **coins bearing a ship** — the emblem of **maritime trade** — with a wide east–west distribution.
- **Decline (3rd c. CE).** The empire fragmented among successors — the **Ikṣvākus** (Andhra, at Nāgārjunakoṇḍa — a Part-B topic), **Ābhīras, Chuṭus**, and the resurgent **Western Kṣatrapas**.

### 2.2 The evidence

- **Inscriptions (the backbone):**
  - **The Naneghat inscription of Queen Nāganikā (Nāyanikā)** — records the **Vedic sacrifices** (rājasūya, aśvamedha) performed by the early Sātavāhanas and their **sacrificial fees (dakṣiṇā)** — evidence both for their **Vedic-Brahmanical** character and for the **high status of royal women**.
  - **The Nāsik praśasti of Gautamī Balaśrī** — the eulogy of Gautamīputra (Śaka-slayer; *eka-brāhmaṇa*).
  - **Nāsik & Karle cave inscriptions** — records of **land and cave grants** to Buddhist monks.
- **Coins:** lead, potin and copper (and some silver) — including the **restruck Nahapāna** coins and the **ship-type** coins of Yajña Śrī — key for chronology, trade and the western/eastern reach.
- **Literature:** **Hāla's *Gāthāsaptaśatī* (Sattasaī)** — a **Prakrit (Māhārāṣṭrī)** anthology of 700 lyric verses attributed to King Hāla, a rare window onto **rural life, women and love**; and **Guṇāḍhya's *Bṛhatkathā*** (in **Paiśācī** Prakrit; lost) traditionally tied to the Sātavāhana court.
- **The *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea*** (§6) — names their western marts.
- **Monuments:** the rock-cut **chaityas and vihāras** of **Karle, Nāsik, Bhaja, Kanheri**; and the **Amaravati / Nāgārjunakoṇḍa** stūpas.

### 2.3 Analysis & debates — the exam payload

- **The first religious land grants → the seed of "Indian feudalism."** The Sātavāhanas were **the first dynasty to grant land — with fiscal and administrative immunities — to Brahmins and Buddhist monks** (e.g., Gautamīputra's tax-free village to the Nāsik monks). Historians (esp. **R.S. Sharma**) treat this **land-grant-with-immunity** as the **germ of the later "feudal" order** that matures under the Guptas (§4.3). *This is the single most analytical point in the section — carry it forward as a through-line.*
- **Brahmanical in ritual, Buddhist in patronage — religious dualism.** Gautamīputra styles himself **"eka-brāhmaṇa"** (the "unique/peerless Brahmin") and claims to have **stopped the mixing of the four varṇas (varṇa-saṃkara)** — i.e., a **champion of the Brahmanical order** — while the same dynasty **lavishly endowed Buddhist caves** (Karle, Nāsik) and stūpas (Amaravati). **Vedic sacrifice + Buddhist endowment under one crown** = a model of early Indian **religious pluralism**.
- **The matronymic puzzle.** Kings bore **metronymics** — **Gautamī**putra ("son of Gautamī"), **Vāsiṣṭhī**putra ("son of Vāsiṣṭhī") — naming the **mother's gotra**. Read cautiously: it signals the **prominence/high descent of royal women** (Nāganikā, Gautamī Balaśrī), but succession remained **patrilineal**; the metronymic likely distinguished sons within a **polygamous** royal house. A favourite one-liner.
- **The Indo-Roman trade peak.** The Sātavāhana Deccan sat astride the **west-coast trade** (ports **Bharukaccha/Broach, Sopārā, Kalyan**) at the **height of Indo-Roman commerce** — Roman coin finds in the Deccan; the **ship-coins**; guilds (**śreṇis**) acting as **bankers** (inscriptions record permanent cash endowments *deposited with guilds*, the interest funding monks). A prosperous, monetised, urban-commercial economy — the material base of the art and the grants.
- **Language & culture.** **Prakrit was the official language** — inscriptions and Hāla's *Sattasaī* are in Prakrit — a marker distinguishing the Sātavāhanas from the **Sanskrit**-favouring dynasties that followed (the Śakas' Rudradāman and then the Guptas). A **Prakrit "golden age"** in the Deccan.
- **Art — the Amaravati school.** The **Amaravati school** (white/greenish limestone; the great **Amaravati stūpa** and **Nāgārjunakoṇḍa** reliefs) is **one of the three seminal early schools of Indian sculpture** — alongside **Gandhāra** and **Mathura** (companion §9.3) — and the **leading South-Indian school**: slender, graceful, dynamic figures, dense narrative relief. (Amaravati is the crown of Andhra art — a Part-B jewel.)

### 2.4 Answer hooks

- Sātavāhanas = **the first great Deccan empire**, the **bridge between north and south**, and the **founding matrix of Andhra history** (Part-B cross-reference).
- **Gautamīputra Sātakarṇi** (Nāsik praśasti of **Gautamī Balaśrī**) = the restorer who **crushed the Śaka Nahapāna** (**Jogalthambi** restrikes) and posed as **eka-brāhmaṇa**, guardian of varṇa-dharma.
- **First land grants with immunities to Brahmins/monks → the seed of "Indian feudalism"** (R.S. Sharma) — the key analytical thread.
- **Vedic sacrifice (Naneghat/Nāganikā) + Buddhist endowment (Karle, Amaravati)** = religious dualism; **metronymic** royal names = prominence of royal women.
- **Prakrit** official language; **Hāla's Gāthāsaptaśatī**; **Indo-Roman trade peak** (ship-coins, guild-banking); **Amaravati school** of art.

## 3. The Sangam Age in South India (c. 3rd c. BCE – 3rd c. CE)

**Significance:** while the Deccan was Sātavāhana, the **Tamil country (Tamilakam)**, south of the Tirupati/Venkaṭam hills, had its own brilliant, largely **pre-Brahmanical** civilisation — a **heroic-age society of chiefdoms** reconstructed mainly from the **oldest literature of any Dravidian tongue**. It integrates the deep south into "Indian history" and gives Tamil its claim to **classical antiquity**.

### 3.1 The core narrative

- **The three crowned kings (mū-vēndar).** Three lineages divided Tamilakam, each with an emblem, capital and great port:

| Dynasty | Emblem | Capital | Chief port | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cheras** | **Bow** | Vanji (**Karur**) | **Muziris (Muciri)** | Kerala + west; the Roman pepper trade |
| **Cholas** | **Tiger** | **Uraiyur** | **Kaviripattinam / Puhar** | Kaveri delta; **Grand Anicut** |
| **Pandyas** | **Fish** | **Madurai** | **Korkai** | Pearls; patrons of the Sangams |

Below them: minor chieftains (**vēḷir**) and the celebrated **"seven great patrons" (kaḍai ezhu vaḷḷal)** of bardic praise.
- **"Sangam" = academy.** Tradition holds that **assemblies (Sangams) of Tamil poets** met under **Pandyan patronage at Madurai**; three Sangams are legendary (the first two "submerged"), and the surviving corpus is that of the **"Third Sangam."**
- **The kings to remember (one each):** Chera **Senguttuvan** — hero of the epic *Cilappatikāram*, founder of the **Paṭṭini cult** (worship of **Kaṇṇaki** as goddess of chastity); Chola **Karikāla** — victor of **Venni**, founder of Puhar, builder of the **Kallanai (Grand Anicut)** across the **Kāveri** (an ancient dam **still in use**); Pandya **Nedunjeliyan** — victor of **Talaiyālaṅgānam**.

### 3.2 The evidence

- **Sangam literature (the primary source).** The **Ettuttokai (Eight Anthologies)** and **Pattuppāṭṭu (Ten Idylls)**; the grammar-cum-poetics **Tolkāppiyam** (oldest extant Tamil work); the didactic **Pathinenkīḻkaṇakku (Eighteen Minor Works)**, which includes the **Tirukkuṛaḷ of Tiruvaḷḷuvar** (strictly a later, post-Sangam ethical classic); and the twin epics **Cilappatikāram (Iḷaṅgo Aḍigaḷ)** and **Maṇimēkalai (Sīttalai Sāttanār)** — themselves **post-Sangam** (c. 5th–6th c.). Poems fall into **akam** (interior/love) and **puṟam** (exterior/war and public life).
- **Foreign & epigraphic anchors (crucial for dating):** **Ashoka's edicts (RE II & XIII)** name the **Choḍas (Cholas), Pāṇḍyas, Keralaputras (Cheras) and Satiyaputras** as free peoples on his southern border — the **earliest datable reference** and a chronological anchor; **Khāravela's Hāthīgumphā** mentions a Tamil confederacy; and the Roman texts (§6) — the **Periplus, Pliny, Ptolemy** — describe the ports and trade.
- **Archaeology:** **Roman coin hoards** (Augustus, Tiberius) across Tamil Nadu; **Arikamedu** near Pondicherry — an **Indo-Roman trading station** (Wheeler's excavation); **Keezhadi/Keeladi** near Madurai — recent excavations pushing back Tamil **urbanism and literacy** (Tamil-Brāhmī); and the **Tamil-Brāhmī cave inscriptions** on Jain/Buddhist "beds."

### 3.3 Analysis & debates

- **The "Sangam problem" (chronology).** The corpus is **not precisely dated**, spans centuries, and the "three Sangams" story is **unhistorical**; scholars bracket the bulk to **c. 100 BCE – 250 CE** using the **Ashokan** and **Roman** synchronisms. *Flag the chronology as reconstructed, never exact.*
- **Polity — chiefdom, not bureaucratic state.** Sangam kingship was a **kin-based, heroic chiefdom** run on **plunder-and-redistribution**: war, booty and the **gift (kodai)** economy, with **bards** trading praise for patronage (the reading associated with **K. Kailasapathy**). The land was seen through the **five tiṇais** (eco-zones — kuṟiñji hills, mullai forest, marudam farmland, neital coast, pālai wasteland), which organise **both landscape and poetic convention**. *Do not describe it as a centralised empire.*
- **Economy — Indo-Roman maritime trade.** The engine was **overseas trade**: pepper, pearls, fine muslin, ivory and gems for **Roman gold**; the ports **Muziris, Puhar, Korkai** and **Arikamedu**; **"Yavana" (Greco-Roman) merchants and even bodyguards** appear in the poems. Agriculture (the Kāveri delta), fishing and weaving underpinned it.
- **Society & religion.** An early Tamil society **before full Brahmanisation**: worship of **Murugaṉ/Seyoṉ** (the red war-god), **Māyoṉ** (Viṣṇu), and the **Paṭṭini/Kaṇṇaki** cult; **hero-stones (naṭukal)** for fallen warriors; the gradual entry of the **varṇa order and Sanskrit ritual** from the north; a woman poet like **Auvaiyār**; harsh **widowhood** and the appearance of **sati**.
- **Language & synthesis.** The Sangam corpus is the **oldest literature of any Dravidian language** and a basis for Tamil's status as a **classical language**; the age also stages the **Aryan–Dravidian synthesis** — northern Brahmanical/Vedic elements fusing with indigenous Tamil culture to seed the later composite south-Indian tradition.

### 3.4 Answer hooks

- Sangam = **Tamilakam under the Cheras (bow/Muziris), Cholas (tiger/Puhar), Pandyas (fish/Madurai)** — a **heroic-age chiefdom** society, reconstructed mainly from **Sangam literature (akam/puṟam)**, anchored by **Ashokan edicts + Roman trade**.
- One king each: **Karikāla** (Grand Anicut on the Kāveri), **Senguttuvan** (Paṭṭini cult / Cilappatikāram), **Nedunjeliyan** (Talaiyālaṅgānam).
- **Indo-Roman trade** = the economic engine (Muziris, Arikamedu, Roman gold, "Yavanas"); **Tolkāppiyam** (grammar); the twin epics **Cilappatikāram & Maṇimēkalai**.
- **Tamil's classical antiquity** and the **Aryan–Dravidian synthesis**; source caveat: the "three Sangams" legend is unhistorical, dating is reconstructed.

## 4. The Guptas (c. 320 – 550 CE) — the "Classical Age"

**Significance:** after ~a century of fragmentation the **Guptas reunified the north** and presided over what nationalist historiography named the **"Golden / Classical Age"** of ancient India — a genuine **zenith in Sanskrit letters, science, mathematics, art and temple religion**. But the age is best examined as a **balance-sheet**: culturally golden, socially a hardening of caste and a decline in women's status, administratively the **beginning of the decentralised, land-grant ("feudal") order**.

### 4.1 The core narrative — the great kings

- **The founders.** **Śrī Gupta** and **Ghaṭotkaca** (title only *mahārāja*) — obscure. **Chandragupta I** (c. 319/320–335) — first **mahārājādhirāja**; married the **Licchavi** princess **Kumāradevī** (a prestige alliance, celebrated on coins); the **Gupta Era (319–320 CE)** dates from him.
- **Samudragupta (c. 335–375) — the conqueror.** Our master source is the **Allahabad (Prayāga) Praśasti** by his court poet **Hariṣeṇa**, incised on an **Ashokan pillar**. It records a graded conquest policy:
  - **Āryāvarta (the north):** **9 kings uprooted and *annexed*.**
  - **Dakṣiṇāpatha (the south):** **12 kings captured and *released*** — **dharma-vijaya** (tribute and submission, not annexation), including **Viṣṇugopa of Kāñcī**.
  - **Frontier kingdoms and republics** paying homage; and **foreign powers** — Śakas, the **Kushāṇa** ("Daivaputra-Ṣāhi"), and **Siṃhala (Sri Lanka)** — in diplomatic submission.
  - He revived the **aśvamedha** (aśvamedha-type coins), and his **vīṇā-playing coins** show a poet-musician king. **V.A. Smith's "Indian Napoleon."**
- **Chandragupta II "Vikramāditya" (c. 375–415) — the apogee.** **Destroyed the Western Kṣatrapas (Śakas of Ujjain/Gujarat)**, annexing **Malwa, Gujarat and Kathiawar** — winning the **western ports and the sea-trade**. Diplomacy by marriage: allied with the **Vākāṭakas** by wedding his daughter **Prabhāvatīguptā** to **Rudrasena II**, projecting Gupta influence into the Deccan. The **Mehrauli Iron Pillar** (of "Chandra") — a **rust-resistant metallurgical marvel** — and the visit of **Fa-Hien** (companion §9A) belong to his reign; tradition seats the **navaratna** ("nine gems," incl. **Kālidāsa**) at his court.
- **Kumāragupta I (c. 415–455)** — held the empire; **founded the Nālandā mahāvihāra** (§7); worshipped **Kārttikeya**; late in his reign the **Hūṇa** threat loomed.
- **Skandagupta (c. 455–467) — the last great king.** **Repelled the Hūṇas (Hephthalites / White Huns)** (the **Bhitari** pillar and **Junāgaḍh** inscription, where he also **repaired the Sudarśana Lake** yet again — a thread running from Candragupta Maurya through Rudradāman). The Hūṇa wars **strained the treasury** (debased gold).
- **Decline (late 5th–6th c.).** Repeated **Hūṇa invasions (Toramāṇa, Mihirakula)**, weak successors, the rise of feudatories (**later Guptas, Maukharis, Maitrakas of Valabhī, Vākāṭakas**), and the drift to **land-grant feudalism** dissolved the empire by **c. 550 CE**.

### 4.2 The evidence

- **Inscriptions:** the **Allahabad Praśasti** (Hariṣeṇa — the source for Samudragupta); the **Mehrauli Iron Pillar**; the **Bhitari & Junāgaḍh** records (Skandagupta/Hūṇas); the **Eran** inscriptions — one of which (**510 CE**) gives the **first epigraphic evidence of sati** in India.
- **Coins:** the superb **Gupta gold dīnāras** — the **finest indigenous gold coinage of ancient India** (building on Kushāṇa gold, companion §9.4), with rich iconography (king with bow, with **vīṇā**, performing aśvamedha; goddesses Lakṣmī/Gaṅgā). *The later **debasement** of the gold is itself the index of decline.*
- **Foreign account:** **Fa-Hien** — the Gupta polity seen from outside (mild rule, ~1/6 revenue, fines over mutilation, charitable hospitals; companion §9A).
- **Literature & science texts** (below) — a source in their own right for the age's mind.

### 4.3 Analysis & debate — "Golden Age": for, and against

**The case FOR (the "Classical Age"):**
- **Political unity & stability** over much of the north for ~150 years.
- **Sanskrit renaissance:** **Kālidāsa** (*Abhijñānaśākuntalam, Meghadūta, Raghuvaṃśa, Kumārasambhava*); **Viśākhadatta** (*Mudrārākṣasa*); the **Pañcatantra**; **Amarasiṃha's** lexicon (*Amarakośa*); the **Purāṇas and epics** reach near-final form. Sanskrit becomes the language of court and high culture (a shift from the Sātavāhana-age Prakrit).
- **Science & mathematics — the true jewel:** **Āryabhaṭa** (*Āryabhaṭīya*, **499 CE**) — earth's rotation, place-value notation, a close value of **π**, trigonometry, the **true cause of eclipses** (not Rāhu/Ketu); **Varāhamihira** (*Bṛhatsaṃhitā*, *Pañcasiddhāntikā*); and, in this milieu, the maturing of the **decimal place-value system and the concept of zero** — arguably India's greatest gift to the world. Medicine (**Vāgbhaṭa**; the Caraka/Suśruta traditions) and metallurgy (the rustless pillar).
- **Art & architecture — the classical canon:** the **free-standing structural temple** is born — early **Nāgara** shrines (the **Deogarh Daśāvatāra** temple; the **Bhitargaon** brick temple; Tigawa; Sāñchī T-17); the **Ajanta** murals (Vākāṭaka–Gupta age — the finest ancient Indian painting); the serene **Sārnāth Buddha** and the great **Mathura/Sārnāth** Gupta sculpture (the "**Gupta idiom**" — the aesthetic benchmark of Indian art); the **Sultanganj bronze Buddha**; the **Udayagiri** caves.
- **Religion:** the flowering of **Puranic, theistic Hinduism** (**Bhāgavata/Vaiṣṇavism**, Śaivism) under royal patronage — the Guptas were **parama-bhāgavatas** with the **Garuḍa** emblem — while remaining **tolerant** (Kumāragupta founded the **Buddhist** Nālandā; Jain patronage continued). Temple worship, the **avatāra** doctrine, image-worship, bhakti-in-embryo.

**The case AGAINST / the qualifications (write these — they mark the analytical candidate):**
- **"Golden for whom?"** The age saw the **hardening of caste and a decline in women's status**: the **first epigraphic sati (Eran, 510 CE)**; **child marriage**, restrictions on widows, denial of property and education; and **untouchability** (Fa-Hien's Chaṇḍāla passage, companion §9A). It was **golden mainly for the upper-caste male elite**.
- **The seeds of feudalism (R.S. Sharma's thesis).** Proliferating **land grants (agrahāra / brahmadeya)** to Brahmins and temples, carrying **fiscal and administrative immunities (parihāra)**, plus a **decline of towns, coinage and long-distance trade** (the debated **post-Gupta urban decay**), point to a **more decentralised, self-sufficient, "feudal" village economy**. Administratively the Gupta state was **far less centralised than the Mauryan**: a hierarchy of **sāmantas (feudatories)**, provinces (**bhuktis**) under **uparikas**, districts (**viṣayas**) under **viṣayapatis**, with strong **local/guild self-government** (the **Dāmodarpur** and other copper-plates show district and town committees sanctioning land sales). *Contrast the centralised Arthaśāstra state of the Mauryas (companion §6.4).*
- **Regionally limited unity.** The "unity" was **north-Indian**; the Deccan and south ran their own (Vākāṭaka, Pallava, Kadamba) courses.

**Balanced verdict:** *culturally and scientifically a genuine classical zenith; socially a period of Brahmanical consolidation and declining status for women and lower castes; administratively the birth of the decentralised, land-grant order later called "feudal."*

### 4.4 Answer hooks

- Gupta = **"Classical Age"**: **Sanskrit** (Kālidāsa), **science** (**Āryabhaṭa; zero & decimals**), **classical temple & sculpture** (Deogarh, Ajanta, Sārnāth Buddha), **Puranic Hinduism** — but qualify with **"golden for whom?"** (**sati at Eran, 510 CE**; caste hardening; women's decline).
- **Samudragupta** (Allahabad Praśasti, Hariṣeṇa) = "Indian Napoleon"; **annexation in the north, dharma-vijaya in the south**; aśvamedha & vīṇā coins.
- **Chandragupta II "Vikramāditya"** = **crushed the Western Śakas** (won the west-coast ports), **Vākāṭaka** marriage (Prabhāvatīguptā), **Mehrauli iron pillar**, host to **Fa-Hien**.
- Administration = **decentralised, sāmanta / land-grant** order → **R.S. Sharma's "Indian feudalism"** seed (contrast the centralised Mauryan state).
- **Skandagupta** = the **Hūṇa-repeller**; Hūṇa wars + **debased gold** = the decline index. Gupta **gold dīnāras** = the finest ancient Indian coinage; **parama-bhāgavata** (Garuḍa) yet tolerant (founded Nālandā).

## 5. Kanauj — Harṣavardhana and the Imperial City (7th c. CE and after)

**Significance:** after the Guptas, north India fragmented, and the **city of Kanauj (Kanyakubja)** rose to become the **coveted seat of northern paramountcy** — "the throne of Āryāvarta." Its first great master, **Harṣavardhana**, is often called **the last great emperor of the north before the Turkish conquest**; but his "empire" was a **loose hegemony of feudatories** that **died with him**, and Kanauj's later career — the **Tripartite Struggle** — shows how the imperial ideal outran anyone's capacity to hold it.

### 5.1 The core narrative

- **The setting.** Post-Gupta north India split among the **Maukharis (of Kanauj)**, the **Later Guptas (Magadha)**, the **Maitrakas (Valabhī, Gujarat)**, the **Puṣyabhūti/Vardhanas (Thāneśvar/Sthāṇvīśvara)**, and the aggressive **Gauḍa (Bengal) king Śaśāṅka** — with the **Hūṇas** receding.
- **The Puṣyabhūti (Vardhana) house.** **Prabhākaravardhana** (first important king) and his children **Rājyavardhana**, **Harṣavardhana**, and **Rājyaśrī** (married to the Maukhari **Grahavarman** of Kanauj).
- **Harṣa's accession (606 CE, the "Harṣa Era").** **Śaśāṅka of Gauḍa** killed **Grahavarman** and then **Rājyavardhana**; the young **Harṣa** took the throne, rescued his sister, and **united Thāneśvar and Kanauj**, shifting his capital to **Kanauj** — the city's rise to premier status dates from here.
- **Extent — and its limit.** Harṣa brought most of the **Gangetic north** under his sway (from the Punjab/Kathiawar to Bengal; alliance with **Bhāskaravarman of Kāmarūpa/Assam**). But he was **decisively checked on the Narmadā** by the **Chālukya Pulakeśin II** (c. 618–619 CE) — the **Aihole inscription of Ravikīrti** records the repulse. **So Harṣa was a great *northern* emperor, not a pan-Indian one — the Deccan barred the door.**
- **After Harṣa.** He left **no heir**; his minister **Arjuna/Arunāśva** usurped and was **defeated by the Chinese–Tibetan mission of Wang Xuance**. Kanauj remained the **prize-symbol** of the north: **Yaśovarman** of Kanauj (8th c., patron of **Bhavabhūti** and **Vākpati**) → the **Tripartite Struggle** for Kanauj among the **Gurjara-Pratihāras, the Pālas (Bengal) and the Rāṣṭrakūṭas (Deccan)** (8th–9th c.), **won by the Pratihāras** (Nāgabhaṭa II, **Mihira Bhoja**), who made Kanauj their imperial capital → finally the **Gāhaḍavālas (Jayachandra)**, defeated by **Muhammad Ghūrī (1194)**. **This long career as the contested imperial city is "the Kanauj and their contributions" of the syllabus.**

### 5.2 The evidence

- **Bāṇabhaṭṭa's *Harṣacarita*** — the **court biography** of Harṣa (the first historical **biography / ākhyāyikā** in Sanskrit) — laudatory and invaluable, but **panegyric**; Bāṇa also wrote the romance **Kādambarī**.
- **Hiuen-Tsang's *Si-yu-ki*** (companion §9A) — the **external eyewitness** of Harṣa's realm: the **quinquennial Prayāga assembly**, the **Kanauj assembly**, revenue (~1/6), roads, society.
- **Harṣa's own works:** the Sanskrit plays **Ratnāvalī, Priyadarśikā** and **Nāgānanda** (a rare **royal author**).
- **Inscriptions & seals:** the **Banskhera** and **Madhuban** copper-plates, the **Sonpat** seal; and — from the *other* side — the **Aihole inscription** recording the Narmadā check.
- **Chinese diplomatic records** (Wang Xuance).

### 5.3 Analysis & debates

- **Administration — feudalism deepens.** Hiuen-Tsang and the land-grant records show a polity resting on **sāmantas (feudatories)** and on **paying officers and priests in land, not cash** — the **feudal tendency of the Gupta age intensifies**. Revenue was **light (~1/6)**; state income was split four ways (administration, salaries, scholars, charity); the king **toured ceaselessly**.
- **Religion — eclectic, Buddhist-leaning.** A **Śaiva** by background, Harṣa grew **sympathetic to Mahāyāna Buddhism** under Hiuen-Tsang, yet remained **tolerant** (patron of **Śiva, Sūrya and the Buddha**); the **Prayāga dāna-assembly** (where he gave away his treasury) and the **Kanauj Buddhist assembly** (marred, per Hiuen-Tsang, by an assassination/arson attempt) are emblematic; he patronised **Nālandā** (§7).
- **The "last northern empire" thesis — with the necessary qualification.** Harṣa is rightly called **the last great ruler of the north before the Turks**, and his reign a **cultural high point** (Bāṇa, Mayūra, Hiuen-Tsang; Nālandā at its zenith). *But* his realm was a **loose hegemony of feudatories, not a centralised state**, and it **collapsed at his death** for want of an institutional succession — a textbook demonstration of the age's **political fragmentation and incipient feudalism**. Kanauj's subsequent history (the Tripartite Struggle) is the same lesson writ large: **an imperial prize no one could permanently hold — the very condition the Turks would exploit after 1000 CE.**

### 5.4 Answer hooks

- **Harṣa (606–647), capital Kanauj** = **the last great northern emperor before the Turks**, reconstructable from **Bāṇa's *Harṣacarita* + Hiuen-Tsang** — but a **loose feudatory hegemony** that **died with him**.
- **Checked on the Narmadā by Pulakeśin II** (**Aihole inscription**) → **not pan-Indian**; the Deccan barred the north.
- Administration = **deepening feudalism** (sāmantas, land-salaries, ~1/6 revenue); religion = **eclectic, Buddhist-leaning** (Prayāga & Kanauj assemblies; Nālandā patron); a **royal author** (*Ratnāvalī, Nāgānanda*).
- **Kanauj as "the imperial city"** = the coveted seat → the **Tripartite Struggle** (**Pratihāras vs. Pālas vs. Rāṣṭrakūṭas**) → **Pratihāra** apogee → **Gāhaḍavālas** → **1194**. The throne of Āryāvarta.

## 6. Historical Accounts of Foreign Travellers (a consolidated source set-piece)

**Why a whole section (and the exam demand):** foreign accounts are the recurring test of the document's core skill — **source-criticism**. The **2023 Paper-II Q1(b)** asked candidates to examine "the socio-religious life and administration of early India **with respect to the writings of Chinese travellers** such as Fa-Hien, Hiuen-Tsang and I-Tsang" — the **Chinese pilgrims are treated in full in companion §9A**; this section **widens the lens** to the **Greek and Roman** accounts and welds the three streams into one method. *Foreign accounts are external, often second-hand and culturally filtered — priceless for chronology and for what insiders take for granted, but never neutral.*

### 6.1 The three streams

**A. Greek & Hellenistic (chronology + the Mauryas):**
- **Skylax** (c. 6th c. BCE, for Darius) — the earliest Greek notice of the Indus.
- **Ktesias** (*Indica*, c. 5th c. BCE), a Persian-court physician — **fabulous and unreliable** (dog-headed men, gold-digging ants) — the **cautionary baseline** for how *not* to read a foreign source.
- **The Alexander historians** — **Nearchus** (the admiral), Onesicritus, Aristobulus — surviving via **Arrian (*Anabasis*, *Indica*), Curtius, Plutarch, Diodorus**: the first **eyewitness** Greek descriptions and the **"sheet-anchor of ancient Indian chronology"** (companion §8.2).
- **Megasthenes** (*Indica*), Seleucid ambassador to **Candragupta Maurya** (c. 300 BCE) — the great source for Mauryan society and **Pāṭaliputra's administration** (the six committees), but with famous distortions — the **"seven castes"** (misreading occupational groups as endogamous castes) and the claim that India **had no slavery** and **no famine**. *Survives only in fragments quoted by Strabo, Arrian and Diodorus* (companion §6.4). Also **Deimachus** (envoy to **Bindusāra**) and **Dionysius** (a Ptolemaic envoy).

**B. Roman (trade + geography):**
- **The *Periplus of the Erythraean Sea*** (an anonymous Greek-Egyptian merchant, **c. 1st c. CE**) — the indispensable **handbook of Indo-Roman maritime trade**: the ports (**Barbaricum, Barygaza/Broach, Muziris**…), the goods, and **monsoon sailing** (associated with **Hippalus**). It is the **economic backbone** of the Sātavāhana (§2) and Sangam (§3) sections.
- **Pliny the Elder** (*Naturalis Historia*, 1st c. CE) — complained of the **drain of Roman gold** to India — external confirmation of the trade balance.
- **Ptolemy** (*Geography*, 2nd c. CE) — a systematic, if distorted, **gazetteer/map** of Indian places and peoples.
- (**Strabo**, the geographer, is the chief **preserver of Megasthenes**.)

**C. Chinese (Buddhist pilgrims — Gupta–Harṣa age):** the fullest early narratives — **treated in detail in companion §9A**; the one-line recap for standalone use:

| Pilgrim | In India | Contemporary ruler | Work | Payload |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fa-Hien** (Faxian) | c. 399–414 | **Chandragupta II** (Gupta) | *Fo-kwo-ki* | Mild Gupta rule; **~1/6 revenue**; fines over mutilation; **first foreign notice of untouchability** (Chaṇḍālas) |
| **Hiuen-Tsang** (Xuanzang) | c. 630–645 | **Harṣa** of Kanauj | *Si-yu-ki* | Nālandā under **Śīlabhadra**; Prayāga & Kanauj assemblies; **roads less safe** (he was robbed) — the post-Gupta slide |
| **I-Tsing** (Yijing; the paper's "I-Tsang") | c. 671–695 | post-Harṣa | *A Record of the Buddhist Religion…* | **Monastery-university** administration; ~10 yrs at Nālandā; the **sea-route via Śrīvijaya** |

*(Add **Sung-Yun**, early 6th c., a minor earlier pilgrim.)*

### 6.2 The method — the real exam payload

Foreign accounts are strongest as:
1. **Chronological anchors** — the Greeks fix the Mauryas; the **Ashokan edicts' "Yona" kings** and the Alexander historians are the **twin sheet-anchors**; the pilgrims date the Guptas/Harṣa.
2. **Trade & economic evidence** — the **Periplus** and **Pliny** for the Indo-Roman commerce that underpins the Deccan and the Tamil south.
3. **An outsider's notice of what insiders normalise** — **caste, untouchability, sati** (the Chaṇḍāla and sati passages are the **earliest external testimony**).

They are weakest on **motive, internal politics, and anything filtered through the writer's purpose**: a **Buddhist pilgrim over-reports Buddhism** and ignores Hindu kingship; a **merchant sees ports, not courts**; **Megasthenes misreads varṇa as caste**; **Ktesias invents**. **Rule: always name the traveller, his date and his purpose, and cross-check him against inscriptions and coins.** That is precisely the methodological rule of §0.

### 6.3 Answer hooks

- Three streams: **Greek (Megasthenes — the Mauryas), Roman (Periplus / Pliny / Ptolemy — trade), Chinese (Fa-Hien / Hiuen-Tsang / I-Tsing — the Gupta–Harṣa age & Buddhism).**
- **Value = chronology + trade + an outsider's notice of caste/sati; limit = bias, purpose, second-hand transmission** (Megasthenes' "seven castes," Ktesias' fables).
- The **Ashokan "Yona" kings** + the **Alexander historians** = the **twin chronological sheet-anchors**.
- For the **Chinese pilgrims in full, see companion §9A** — this section adds the **Greek and Roman** witnesses and the unifying method.

## 7. Early Educational Institutions

**The arc:** from the **oral, domestic gurukula** of the Vedic world to the great **residential monastic universities (mahāvihāras)** of the Gupta–Pāla age — India as an **educational hub of Asia**. A neat structural point: **almost everything we know of these universities comes from the foreign travellers of §6** (Hiuen-Tsang, I-Tsing) — so the last two syllabus items are **two sides of one coin**.

### 7.1 The Brahmanical / indigenous base

- **The gurukula** — residential, **guru–śiṣya**, oral memorisation of the **Vedas** (śruti); the **pariṣad** and **pāṭhaśālā**.
- **Endowed learning** — **agrahāra / brahmadeya** villages granted to learned Brahmins; **ghaṭikās** (Brahmanical colleges attached to temples, especially in the south — e.g., the **Kāñcī** ghaṭikā under the Pallavas) and **maṭhas**. *Because it was **decentralised** (villages, temples, maṭhas, households), Brahmanical learning proved far more **survivable** than the great centralised Buddhist universities (§7.3).*
- **Curriculum:** the four **Vedas**, the six **Vedāṅgas**, grammar (**Pāṇini**), logic, law (dharmaśāstra), and the **eighteen vidyās / śilpas**.

### 7.2 The great residential universities (mahāvihāras)

| Institution | Founder / patron | Region | Character & fame | End |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Takṣaśilā (Taxila)** | pre-Buddhist; Gandhāra | NW (Pakistan) | **Earliest** centre (c. 6th c. BCE) — **not** a residential university but a **cluster of star teachers**; alumni/associations: **Pāṇini, Kauṭilya/Cāṇakya, Jīvaka** (medicine); law, medicine, military science, the 18 arts | declined with the NW invasions |
| **Nālandā** | **Kumāragupta I** (Gupta); expanded by **Harṣa** & the **Pālas** | Magadha (Bihar) | The **apex Mahāyāna university**; **~10,000 students, ~1,500–2,000 teachers**; a **gate-debate** entrance (only 2–3 in 10 admitted); free board from **~100–200 endowed villages**; a vast library (**Dharmagañja**); teachers **Śīlabhadra, Dignāga, Dharmapāla, Dharmakīrti, Śāntarakṣita, Atīśa** | **destroyed c. 1193 by Bakhtiyar Khalji** |
| **Vikramaśīla** | **Dharmapāla** (Pāla) | Magadha (Bihar) | Rivalled Nālandā; **Tantra/Vajrayāna** and logic; the **dvāra-paṇḍita** system; head **Atīśa Dīpaṅkara Śrījñāna** (shaped Tibetan Buddhism) | destroyed by the Turks c. 1200 |
| **Valabhī (Vallabhī)** | **Maitrakas** | Gujarat | Great centre of **Hīnayāna/Theravāda** and **secular** studies (law, administration, medicine); **I-Tsing ranks it with Nālandā**; drew students seeking government careers | declined c. 8th c. |
| **Odantapurī, Somapura, Jagaddala** | **Pālas** | Bihar / Bengal | Pāla-age mahāvihāras; **Somapura (Pāharpur)** the largest monastery (a UNESCO site) | destroyed by the Turks |

- **Southern & other centres:** **Kāñcīpuram** (the Pallava ghaṭikā — "the Nālandā of the south"); the **Odisha "diamond triangle"** — **Puṣpagiri, Ratnagiri, Lalitagiri, Udayagiri**; the **Andhra Buddhist centres of Nāgārjunakoṇḍa and Amarāvatī** (Ikṣvāku/Sātavāhana — a **Part-B cross-reference**); **Śāradā-pīṭha** (Kashmir); **Mithilā** (later, the home of Nyāya logic); **Ujjayinī** (astronomy — Varāhamihira); **Vārāṇasī** (Brahmanical learning).

### 7.3 Analysis & debates

- **Sources = the foreign travellers.** Our knowledge of Nālandā, Vikramaśīla and Valabhī rests almost wholly on **Hiuen-Tsang and I-Tsing** (§6 / companion §9A), plus **inscriptions/seals** (the Nālandā seals) and later **Tibetan histories** (Tāranātha). *The "foreign travellers" and "educational institutions" items literally depend on each other.*
- **From oral to institutional — funded by land.** Education moved from the **oral, domestic gurukula** to the **institutional, residential mahāvihāra**, endowed by **royal land grants** — tying learning to the **same land-grant economy** that drove Gupta–Harṣa "feudalism" (§4.3, §5.3).
- **India as an educational hub of Asia.** These were **international** universities — students from **China, Korea, Tibet and Southeast Asia** — an early instance of India's cultural export (and the reason Hiuen-Tsang and I-Tsing came at all).
- **Destruction and the fate of Buddhism.** The **Turkish conquests (Nālandā, Vikramaśīla, Odantapurī, c. 1193–1200)** were a **civilisational rupture** and a major cause of **Buddhism's disappearance from India** (companion §5.3) — because Indian Buddhism had become **concentrated in these monastic universities**, destroying them destroyed its institutional base. **Decentralised Brahmanical learning, by contrast, survived** — the structural contrast to close an answer on.

### 7.4 Answer hooks

- Arc: **oral gurukula / agrahāra (Brahmanical) → the great residential Buddhist mahāvihāras** (Nālandā, Vikramaśīla, Valabhī), funded by **royal land grants**.
- **Takṣaśilā** = earliest — a **cluster of star teachers** (Pāṇini, Cāṇakya, Jīvaka), *not* a residential university.
- **Nālandā** = **Kumāragupta's** foundation, the Mahāyāna apex; **Hiuen-Tsang & I-Tsing** the witnesses (10,000 students, the **gate-debate**, endowed villages, the **Dharmagañja** library); **Bakhtiyar Khalji burned it c. 1193.**
- **Vikramaśīla** (Dharmapāla; Tantra; **Atīśa**); **Valabhī** (Hīnayāna + secular; **I-Tsing ranks it with Nālandā**); Pāla **Somapura/Odantapurī**; southern **Kāñcī**; Andhra **Nāgārjunakoṇḍa/Amarāvatī** (Part-B link).
- The clincher: **our evidence is the foreign pilgrims** (the two syllabus items are one), and the **Turkish destruction of the universities helped end Indian Buddhism**, while **decentralised Brahmanical learning survived**.

## 8. Synthesis — the through-lines to carry into the hall

These sections extend the four master-threads of the companion note's §10. Connect them, and an examiner sees a historian, not a list-maker.

1. **From imperial unity to regional states to classical reunion to feudal fragmentation.** The pan-Indian **Maurya** breaks into **regional powers** (Śuṅga north, Sātavāhana Deccan, Sangam south); the **Guptas** reunify the north into a **classical age**; the post-Gupta north **fragments** again around the coveted **Kanauj** (Harṣa, then the Tripartite Struggle). *Unity in ancient India is episodic; fragmentation is the norm to be explained.*

2. **The birth of "Indian feudalism" — the master analytical thread.** **Sātavāhana first land grants with immunities** → **Gupta agrahāra proliferation + declining towns and coinage** → **Harṣa's sāmanta-and-land-salary polity**. Trace this one line and you have the deepest structure of the whole period (R.S. Sharma). *If you write one causal sentence across these sections, write this one.*

3. **Legitimation keeps re-inventing itself.** **Śuṅga/Sātavāhana Vedic sacrifice revived (aśvamedha, Naneghat)** → **Gupta Puranic-Vaiṣṇava temple kingship + the praśasti** → **Harṣa's eclectic dāna-assemblies**. Each age finds a new **software of rule**.

4. **The evidence dictates the argument.** **Praśastis** (Nāsik, Allahabad, Aihole) give an official self-image; **coins** (Sātavāhana lead/ship, Gupta gold dīnāras) give chronology and iconography; **literature** shifts tongue with power (**Prakrit** *Sattasaī* → **Sanskrit** Kālidāsa/Bāṇa; **Tamil** Sangam); **foreign accounts** (Periplus, Fa-Hien, Hiuen-Tsang) give an outsider's biased notice. **Name the source, state its bias** — every time.

5. **The peninsula joins "Indian history."** The Sātavāhana Deccan bridge, the Sangam Tamil south, and the Deccan (Pulakeśin II) **checking** the north together make this a genuinely **subcontinental** story — no longer a Gangetic monologue.

In the three verified papers, **Part-A, Unit-1, Question 1** fell on the **Harappan / Buddhism–Mahāvīra / Chinese-travellers** cluster — all covered in the **companion file** (§2.3, §2.6, §2.7, §5, §9A). Of *these seven* topics, only **"Historical accounts of foreign travellers"** was **directly** tested in Part A:

- **2023, Q1(b) (verified):** "Examine the **socio-religious life and administration of early India with respect to the writings of Chinese travellers** such as Fa-Hien, Hiuen-Tsang and I-Tsang." *(10)* → answered at **companion §9A**, widened here at **§6**.

**But two of these topics are all but guaranteed elsewhere on the paper:**
- **The Sātavāhanas are a Part-B (Andhra) staple** — **2020 Q6(a)** (agrarian system under the Sātavāhanas), **2023 Q6(a)** (socio-religious conditions, women), **2025 Q7** (origins of the Andhras/Andhra language). *Prepare §2 for Part B as well as Part A.*
- **The Guptas and Kanauj are tested *through* the foreign-travellers lens** — the 2023 Chinese-travellers question is answered on **Chandragupta II (Gupta)** and **Harṣa (Kanauj)**, so §4 and §5 are the substance behind that verified question.

### 9.2 High-probability model questions for the untested topics *(NOT past papers — framed in APPSC's verbs)*

- **Śuṅgas:** "The Śuṅga period represented a Brahmanical revival rather than a persecution of Buddhism.' Critically examine."
- **Sātavāhanas (all-India):** "Assess the contribution of the Sātavāhanas to Deccan polity, trade and art, and examine their role as a bridge between north and south India." / "Examine the Sātavāhana land grants as the earliest seeds of Indian feudalism."
- **Sangam Age:** "Reconstruct the polity, economy and society of the Sangam age from its literary and archaeological sources, and evaluate the reliability of that evidence."
- **Guptas:** "'The Gupta age was a golden age — but golden for whom?' Critically evaluate." / "Analyse the administrative and economic changes of the Gupta age that historians read as the beginnings of Indian feudalism."
- **Kanauj / Harṣa:** "Examine Harṣavardhana's achievements and the character of his empire; why is he called the last great emperor of the north?" / "Trace the rise of Kanauj as the imperial city of early-medieval north India."
- **Foreign travellers:** "Evaluate the Greek, Roman and Chinese accounts as sources for early Indian history, indicating their value and their limitations." *(the source-criticism question the paper favours)*
- **Early educational institutions:** "Discuss the character and contribution of the early educational institutions of ancient India (Takṣaśilā, Nālandā, Vikramaśīla, Valabhī), and explain why the Buddhist universities perished while Brahmanical learning survived."

### 9.3 Pattern takeaways

1. **The examiner's verbs are analytical** — *critically examine, assess, evaluate, analyse* — so lead with the **answer hooks and debates**, not narration.
2. **Source-criticism recurs** (foreign travellers; praśastis; Sangam literature) — name the source and state its bias, as §6 and §8 stress.
3. **The Sātavāhanas double as Part B** — the single highest-value dynasty here for APPSC.
4. **"Golden-age" and "feudalism" are the two set-piece debates** on the Guptas — prepare both sides.
